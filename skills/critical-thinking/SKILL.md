# Critical Thinking: Ten Questions for Evaluating Arguments

> Methodology based on Neil Browne & Stuart Keeley, *Asking the Right Questions*; companion to the `dialectical-thinking` skill for *Beyond Either/Or*.

## Positioning and mindset

- **Sponge vs. panning for gold**: the sponge absorbs information effortlessly but can't rank what's worth believing; panning for gold interacts with the material — asking questions, comparing claims against evidence, deciding for yourself what merits belief. This skill is the panning method: read while asking "what justifies this?"
- **Weak-sense vs. strong-sense critical thinking**: weak-sense critique defends views you already hold and destroys arguments you already dislike; strong-sense critique turns the same scrutiny on your own commitments. Someone who only does the former has learned rhetoric, not thinking.
- **Practice with the right values**: autonomy (self-governed judgment), curiosity, humility (acknowledge mistakes), respect for good reasoning wherever found. Two opposing attitudes make the method worthless: emotional attachment to your current beliefs, and the belief that no one-sided answer exists so "anything goes."
- **Domain of applicability**: this skill handles questions of fact, evidence, and argument with checkable truth values (the "ordered domain"). If the question on the table is a long-lived tension where both sides are right (exploit vs. explore, control vs. autonomy), switch to the `dialectical-thinking` skill.
- **The full closed loop**: this skill is the input filter; the `dialectical-thinking` skill reframes genuine tensions; this skill returns to stress-test and pre-mortem the result. Only conflicts that survive fact-checking deserve dialectical integration; the handoff is detailed below.

## The ten-question procedure

For any argument (article, report, speech, ad, news item, social media post, colleague's opinion), run the following ten questions in order. Use them as speed bumps, not a bypass: the goal is to slow down and engage, not to mechanically fill a form.

### 1. What are the issue and the conclusion?
- First ask "what does the author want me to believe or accept?" Clues for locating the conclusion: indicator words (therefore, so, it follows that, shows that, in short, the truth is, clearly), the title, the opening and closing paragraphs, the caption, and the author's own answer to a question posed at the start.
- Classify the issue: a **descriptive issue** (what is the case / what will happen — "do vitamin supplements improve memory?") vs. a **prescriptive issue** (what should be done / what is right — "should universities abolish legacy admissions?"). Prescriptive issues hide value conflicts; expect Question 4 to matter more there.
- Instructional materials vs. arguments: summaries, dictionaries, and encyclopedia entries only state claims without defending them. No conclusion-plus-reasons found → the material is information or emotional expression, not an argument; note that and stop before critiquing it as if it were one.
- Yours too: when writing your own argument, state the issue and flag your conclusion explicitly so the audience isn't left inferring it.

### 2. What are the reasons?
- A conclusion by itself is not evidence. **Reasons are the statements the arguer offers to support the conclusion — the moment a claim is combined with reasons, it becomes an argument.** Indicator words: because, since, as shown by, studies show, given that, the evidence is, for the following reasons.
- Before evaluating, faithfully restate the conclusion and reasons in your own words — this prevents strawmanning and is the single best test of whether you actually understood the argument. If you cannot restate it clearly, the argument itself is unclear.
- Pay attention to **the reason structure**: chains of dependent reasons vs. independent parallel reasons. Independent reasons can stand alone; dependent ones are only as strong as the weakest link — attack the weak link rather than refuting one of several independent supports.
- A bare assertion with no reasons → treat it as a personal opinion, not a fact. Ask for reasons politely: "That's an interesting claim — what makes you believe it?"

### 3. Which words or phrases are ambiguous?
- Focus on abstract words that appear **in the reasons and the conclusion** — their meaning decides whether the reasons support anything at all. Only ambiguity that affects your acceptance of the argument matters.
- Ask three things: which terms could reasonably be understood in more than one way; whether the author clarifies them; how each alternative meaning changes the argument's strength. Substitute each candidate meaning and check whether the conclusion still follows.
- **Two kinds of ambiguity**: semantic (a word with multiple senses — "depression," "freedom") and syntactic (a sentence with multiple parses — "The police shot the rioters with rifles"). Advertisers, politicians, and inspirational writers deliberately exploit ambiguous slogans ("pure," "natural," "relieves pain fast") because each reader supplies their own flattering meaning.
- Beware emotionally loaded terms (fair, freedom, sacrifice, greedy, elite, expert) — polysemy is often exploited to stir feelings rather than make a case. **Emotional terms are often defined by their emotional association with the reader rather than by what the writer has in mind.**
- Unresolved ambiguity makes the argument impossible to evaluate: "if you don't know what a word means, you cannot tell whether the statement containing it is true or false." Request clarification, and be suspicious when a writer is unwilling to clarify.

### 4. What are the value assumptions and descriptive assumptions?
- **Value assumptions**: unstated value priorities that bridge the gap between reasons and prescriptive conclusions. An arguer who prefers individual responsibility over collective duty, or security over liberty, will select reasons accordingly. Identify the **value conflict** (e.g., individual convenience vs. community safety, tradition vs. innovation, free speech vs. harm prevention) and ask: which side does the author favor? Do I share that priority?
- Value preferences are not arguments — but knowing them lets you judge whether the reasons actually justify the conclusion *for you*, and whether a clash is truly about reasons or merely about which side of a genuine tension someone stands on (if the latter, that's a job for the `dialectical-thinking` skill).
- **Descriptive assumptions**: unstated beliefs about how the world works (e.g., "users won't change behavior after the price rises," "what worked before will keep working," "people respond to incentives but not to appeals"). Test each: is it plausible? Does the argument collapse if it fails? Can I imagine a world where the reasons are true but the conclusion is false?
- Practice both directions: when *making* your own argument, list your own value and descriptive assumptions before someone else exposes them.

### 5. Are there any fallacies in the reasoning?
Reasons can be true yet still fail to genuinely support the conclusion — usually because hidden tricks fill the gap. Scan for these moves:

| Fallacy | Signature |
| --- | --- |
| Ad hominem (circumstantial / personal attack) | Attacking the arguer's character or motives instead of the argument ("she works for them, so of course she says that") |
| Slippery slope | Assuming step one inevitably triggers a disaster chain, with no evidence for each individual link |
| Straw man | Distorting an opponent's view into an easily attacked caricature, then demolishing the caricature |
| Appeal to popularity | "Everyone believes it / everyone does it" offered as evidence |
| Appeal to questionable authority | An "expert" whose credentials don't match the topic, has conflicts of interest, or speaks beyond the peer consensus |
| Appeal to tradition / novelty | "It's always been done this way" / "newer is automatically better" |
| Appeal to emotion (fear, pity, anger, hope) | Stirring feelings to lower the audience's reasoning standards; wishful thinking is a close cousin |
| Halo effect | A positive (or negative) label — celebrity, prestige, physical attractiveness, ideology — drowning out the specific argument |
| Begging the question / circular reasoning | Using the conclusion (or a disguised restatement of it) as its own reason |
| Red herring / diversion | Answering a different question, or refocusing the debate on the arguer's pet issue |
| False dilemma / either-or framing | Compressing many live options into two, usually one good and one absurd |
| Perfectionist fallacy | "It won't fix everything, so do nothing" |
| Equivocation | Quietly changing a key word's meaning mid-argument |
| Explaining away / special pleading | Demanding rigorous standards of evidence from opponents while granting yourself exemptions ("my experience is different") |
| Hasty generalization | Stretching a vivid instance or a small sample into a universal claim about a whole group |
| Guilt / virtue by association | Judging a claim by the company of those who endorse it |

Test formula: **when the reasons are true, do they actually support this conclusion? Is a suspicious assumption hidden in between? Would I accept this reasoning if it led to a conclusion I dislike?** (Reversing the direction of application is the strongest single protection against being fooled.)

### 6. How good is the evidence?
Claims need support; evidence varies enormously in quality. Audit each source class with its specific checklist:

- **Intuition / personal experience**: an isolated case; the biggest traps are survivorship bias (counting the winners, never the silent losers) and the availability heuristic (vivid, recent, dramatic instances dominate memory). Vivid ≠ typical; ask for the base rate.
- **Personal stories and typical cases**: strong emotional pull, weak representativeness — that's exactly why persuaders lead with them. Cases can illustrate a possibility, never establish a frequency.
- **Testimonials and endorsements**: selectivity (the endorser was hand-picked), self-interest (paid promotion, personal stake, faith investment in the choice), omitted information (the failures, the non-responders), and the human tendency to believe what we're told by likable people. Ask: who was approached, who declined, who was edited out?
- **Appeals to authority**: checks — does the credential match the claim's domain; is there a conflict of interest; is the authority reporting individual opinion or peer consensus; does the authority have a track record of predictions that were later checked; and does "media authority" (journalists, influencers) actually chain back to a qualified primary source?
- **Personal observation**: observer effects (subjects change behavior when watched), memory reconstruction, motivated bias, and declining attention over long sessions. Multiple independent observers, blind conditions, and instrumentation beat any single eyewitness.
- **Scientific research**: the strongest class, but audit it — sample size and representativeness (who did the study omit?); random assignment and control groups; confounding variables; correlation vs. causation; measurement validity (does the questionnaire item measure what it claims?); ecological validity (lab task vs. real-world behavior); publication bias and funder interests (industry-funded results deserve extra scrutiny); replicability — **a single study is not a verdict**; prefer meta-analyses and systematic reviews. "Studies show" demands: which study, of what quality, replicated by whom?
- **Analogies**: reason by similarity — "the two share attributes A and B, so they probably share C too." Test the structure: is the number of similarities large, and are the similarities **causally relevant** to the conclusion? Are there more important dissimilarities? (Every analogy fails at some point; the question is whether the disanalogy touches the very mechanism at issue.)
- **Ratings, rankings, and surveys** (a modern staple): who produced the ranking, by what criteria, who funded it, and are the criteria the ones you care about? For surveys: question wording and order effects, response-rate coverage, and social desirability bias ("what people say" ≠ "what people do").

### 7. Are there rival causes?
- **Correlation is not causation**; happening before is not causing. When one event follows another, the "after this, therefore because of this" inference (post hoc) is a hypothesis, not a finding.
- Systematically check the rivals: A→B, B→A (reverse causation), C causing both A and B (common cause / confounding), selection bias (only certain kinds of people ended up in the sample), and coincidence. For group differences: is the comparison group genuinely comparable?
- Ask: "what other explanation would fit the facts just as well?" and "which explanation requires the fewest unsupported assumptions?" Control groups, natural experiments, and before/after designs are the decisive tools.
- Be especially suspicious when the discovered cause is also the one the discoverer benefits from politically, commercially, or emotionally — motivated explanation plagues this step.
- A single cause explaining a complex social phenomenon → highly suspicious; complex outcomes usually have interacting multiple causes. Before accepting an explanation, compare the evidence strength across several candidates.
- Toward your own conclusions: generate at least one rival explanation for your preferred causal story before finalizing any analysis.

### 8. Are the statistics deceptive?
- **Relative vs. absolute risk**: "the risk doubles" may mean from one in a million to two in a million. Always ask for the base rate behind any relative change.
- **The missing denominator**: a percentage without its base is meaningless ("90% of our users are satisfied" — out of how many polled, selected how, by whom?).
- **The averages trap**: mean/median/mode used interchangeably; with a skewed distribution the mean lies. "The average customer" may describe nobody.
- **Suspicious precision and unverifiable numbers**: over-precise figures manufacture false credibility; some numbers are unknowable by anyone (global psychological aggregates) or borderline (vague extrapolations) — treat both as estimates with unknown error bars, not facts.
- **Contradiction check**: does the number cohere with other known figures about the same phenomenon (a market-size estimate larger than the industry's total revenue is a red flag)?
- Ask: to judge this number, what key figures are still missing (comparison baseline, side-effect rate, cost, time range, the raw count behind the percentage)?

### 9. What significant information is omitted?
Almost every argument is an act of selective presentation; advertisements and advocacy omit almost everything that doesn't help the cause. Actively demand the omitted material:
- The most common omissions: **counterarguments and the strongest opposing evidence**; full definitions; the value context (whose interests are served, who pays the costs); the origin of claims (which study, which primary source, when); selection procedures (who was polled or studied, and who was left out); contextual factors and realistic alternatives; **negative effects, risks, and costs** (every proposal for action has downsides; the absence of any stated cost is itself a red flag); the frame's reverse side (e.g., a change reported as lives saved is simultaneously a change reported as lives lost — always ask how the same fact would read in the opposite frame).
- For official-sounding or PR prose, restore the five elements: **[who], [when], [why], [which metric deviated by how much], [who fixes it by what date]**. Posture verbs ("we take this seriously," "work is advancing steadily") → demand physical deliverables and acceptance dates; fuzzy quantifiers ("broadly stable," "largely under control") → demand the warning-line percentage and the denominator; passive voice and vanished subjects ("mistakes were made") → name the agent.
- The writer's perspective, background, and financial stake predict the pattern of omissions. Ask: who gains if I believe this, and what would this person never voluntarily tell me?

### 10. What reasonable conclusions are possible?
- Resist **dichotomous thinking**: the same reasons usually support a family of **conditional conclusions** ("if X holds, then …") rather than one unique verdict. Real-world issues are gray-shaded; reasons rarely point to a single mandatory conclusion.
- Generate alternatives systematically: swap in a different assumption and derive the conclusion it supports; combine the conclusion with a qualifier (time, scope, condition) and check whether a more modest conclusion is the one the reasons can actually carry.
- Conclusion strength must match reason strength: when the evidence is insufficient, "cannot yet be determined" is more accurate than a forced verdict — and more credible.
- Ending at the destination: deciding what to believe is the payoff of the whole procedure. State your conclusion explicitly, flag its conditions, and say what new evidence would change your mind (this also feeds the Popper test in the `dialectical-thinking` skill).

## Applying the two skills together

The ten questions are the input filter; they cannot dissolve a tension where both sides hold true claims. Run the loop:
1. **Input**: critical thinking cleans the facts — fake data, fallacies, equivocation, and unquantified spin get removed before anything else.
2. **Frame**: what remains are genuine, evidence-backed conflicts (real R&D constraints vs. real market windows; real elder-care needs vs. real first-floor losses in an elevator dispute). Switch to `dialectical-thinking` for diagnosis (four tension types), reframing, splitting, prioritization, and third-option design.
3. **Output**: critical thinking returns — pre-mortem the new solution, check its hidden assumptions, cost accounting, falsification conditions, and omitted risks before committing.

## Output conventions

- Respond in the user's language; keep established English terms when they are clearer.
- When evaluating an argument: first restate its issue, conclusion, and reasons in one or two sentences (proving accurate understanding, avoiding a straw man), then present findings per the ten questions, ordered by severity (fatal flaws > evidential weaknesses > presentation issues).
- Give an overall grade in three levels: **argument holds / holds with reservations / does not hold**, and name the decisive gap.
- Be constructive: with every problem identified, attach "to repair this argument, the author would need to supply …"
- Practice strong-sense critique: run the ten questions on your own conclusion too; write out the falsification condition for your key judgment (by what date, at what metric, would I admit I was wrong).
- Mind the tone: ask questions rather than deliver verdicts ("what evidence supports this?" beats "that's wrong"); critique reasoning, never persons.
- When a genuine conflict cannot be dissolved by fact-checking (two legitimate claims), say explicitly "this is where `dialectical-thinking` should take over" — do not force a black-and-white verdict.
