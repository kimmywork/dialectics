Part II

Chapter 3 Zeno and the Mojing: Using Reductio ad Absurdum and Paradoxes to Expose Conceptual Flaws

How to Stress-Test Absolutist Slogans by Following the Opponent's Logic

In the first two chapters, we learned to distinguish the four types of tension and saw that some debates stall because the question itself has stepped out of bounds. Yet there is another common situation: a problem lies squarely within empirical bounds, and someone advances a claim that sounds plausible—even self-evident—to everyone in the room (such as “The more granular our KPI metrics, the harder employees will work,” or “Every opinion in the world is just a personal bias”).

Your gut tells you something is wrong with that claim, but if you argue against it head-on, the other person has a hundred ready-made comebacks waiting for you. That is when the sharpest scalpel in the dialectical toolkit comes into play: Follow your opponent’s premise down its own path until its logic slams into a brick wall.

In the West, this scalpel is Zeno’s reductio ad absurdum; in pre-Qin China, it is the Mohist School’s self-referential analysis of “All speech is perverse” (yan jin bei 言尽悖).

3.1 Zeno’s Reductio ad Absurdum: Following an Opponent’s Logic to an Absurd Conclusion

In the fifth century BCE, Zeno of Elea designed a suite of paradoxes in defense of his teacher Parmenides that baffled Greek thinkers for two millennia, the most famous being Achilles and the Tortoise and The Flying Arrow Is at Rest.

  • Achilles and the Tortoise: Achilles, the fleetest hero in Greece, races a tortoise that has a 100-meter head start. Even if Achilles runs ten times faster than the tortoise, Zeno argued that Achilles can never overtake it: by the time Achilles runs the 100 meters to reach Point A where the tortoise started, the tortoise has crawled 10 meters ahead to Point B; by the time Achilles runs those 10 meters to reach Point B, the tortoise has crawled 1 meter ahead to Point C… Ad infinitum, whenever Achilles reaches where the tortoise was, the tortoise has moved a tiny fraction of a millimeter ahead. Because there are infinitely many catch-up stages to traverse, Achilles can never catch the tortoise.
  • The Flying Arrow Is at Rest: At every single instant of its flight (a durationless “now”), a shot arrow occupies a fixed position in space equal to its own volume. Since at each individual instant it is motionless in that spot, summing together an infinite number of motionless instants means the arrow is motionless throughout its entire flight.

Anyone with an ounce of common sense knows that Achilles sprints past the tortoise in seconds and that a flying arrow is anything but still. Was Zeno an idiot who lacked basic common sense?

Quite the opposite. Aristotle called Zeno the “inventor of dialectic” because Zeno demonstrated a surgical technique of conceptual deconstruction: reductio ad absurdum (reduction to absurdity).

The procedure has just three steps:

  1. Temporarily Grant the Opponent’s Premise: Instead of directly attacking your premise PP, I assume for the sake of argument that PP is 100% true;
  2. Deduce Strictly Along That Premise: Using valid logical steps, push premise PP to an extreme boundary condition or universal application to derive its necessary consequence QQ;
  3. Expose the Absurdity of the Consequence: Everyone sees that consequence QQ either clashes head-on with undeniable empirical fact or contradicts premise PP itself. Since QQ is absurd, everyone is forced to look back and admit: That seemingly self-evident initial premise PP must harbor a hidden crack.

Zeno did not actually believe a runner cannot pass a tortoise in the street; he used the deduction to force Greek thinkers to see that our everyday, unexamined notions of “time, space, motion, and infinite divisibility” contained severe logical fissures (such as assuming that the sum of infinitely many diminishing time intervals must be infinite rather than finite). It took humanity two thousand years—culminating in limit theory and calculus—to patch the conceptual cracks Zeno exposed.

Deep Dive The Complete Logical Chain of Zeno’s Four Paradoxes of Motion and Conceptual Crack Diagnosis

Popular accounts usually mention only “Achilles and the Tortoise” and “The Flying Arrow,” but Zeno actually constructed a four-way pincer movement—The Four Paradoxes of Motion—around a single fundamental question: Are space and time composed of infinitely divisible continuous intervals, or of indivisible discrete points? Reading all four together reveals the full power of master-level reductio:

  1. The First Paradox: The Dichotomy (Targeting the Assumption of Continuous Infinite Divisibility)
    • Deduction: You want to walk from starting point AA to destination BB. Before reaching BB, you must first traverse half the distance (1/21/2); before traversing that half, you must traverse half of that half (1/41/4), and before that, 1/81/8, 1/161/16… If space is infinitely divisible, you must complete an infinite number of steps before taking even your first stride. Since an infinite number of distinct actions cannot be completed in a finite time, motion can never even begin!
  2. The Second Paradox: Achilles and the Tortoise (Targeting the Process of Relative Pursuit)
    • Deduction: Even granting that motion has begun, the faster runner can never overtake the slower one, because each time the pursuer reaches the pursued’s previous point, the pursued has advanced a fraction further. Infinite spatial divisibility creates an infinite series of stages, so the pursuit can never terminate.
  3. The Third Paradox: The Arrow (Targeting the Assumption That Time Consists of Discrete Durationless Instants)
    • Deduction: What if the opponent pivots and says, “Space and time are not infinitely divisible continua; they are made of indivisible minimum instants (atoms of time) and minimum spatial points”? Zeno immediately blocks that exit with the Flying Arrow: inside an indivisible, durationless instant of time, no elapsed time exists for the arrow to change position, so it can only occupy a single fixed spatial slot (i.e., be at rest). If time is merely a string of durationless instants, then 0+0+0+…=00 + 0 + 0 + \dots = 0—adding stillness to stillness can never produce motion!
  4. The Fourth Paradox: The Stadium / Moving Rows (Targeting Relative Motion Across Minimum Units of Space and Time)
    • Deduction: Suppose there exists a minimum indivisible unit of time tt and a minimum indivisible unit of spatial distance ss. On a track stand three rows of bodies: Row AA is stationary, Row BB moves right at the rate of “1 spatial unit ss per minimum time unit tt,” and Row CC moves left at the same rate. Within 1 minimum time unit tt, Row BB passes 1 unit of stationary Row AA, but passes 2 units of oncoming Row CC! That means the time it took Row BB to pass 1 unit of Row CC is half of a minimum time unit (t/2t/2)—directly refuting the premise that tt is the smallest indivisible unit of time!

Admire the ruthless geometry of Zeno’s four-part trap: He first asks his opponent: “Do you hold that space and time are infinitely divisible continua, or composed of indivisible minimum quanta?”

  • If the opponent picks “infinitely divisible continua,” Zeno deploys Paradoxes 1 and 2 (Dichotomy and Achilles) to show that infinite division makes motion impossible to start or finish;
  • If the opponent picks “indivisible minimum quanta,” Zeno deploys Paradoxes 3 and 4 (The Arrow and The Stadium) to show that minimum instants either freeze the arrow in midair or get sliced in half during relative motion!

Whichever way the opponent turns, their naive intuitive concepts of space, time, and motion leak like a sieve. Only in the nineteenth century, when Augustin-Louis Cauchy and Karl Weierstrass established rigorous limit theory—proving that the limit of the partial sums of the infinite series 12+14+18+…+12n\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \dots + \frac{1}{2^n} converges to the finite constant 11, and defining instantaneous velocity not as a static position but as the derivative of position with respect to time dsdt\frac{ds}{dt}—did humanity finally seal the conceptual breach Zeno had blown open.

That is how dialectical thinking treats contradictions and paradoxes: A paradox is not a parlor trick for winning barroom arguments; it is a smoke detector for your conceptual system. Where there is smoke, an underlying assumption is short-circuiting.

3.2 The Mojing on Self-Contradiction (Bei): “To Claim All Speech Is Perverse Is Perverse” and Self-Referential Analysis

While Zeno excelled at pushing an opponent’s premise outward to its extreme, the Later Mohist school (Houqi Mojia) in Warring States China invented an even more compact, one-strike reductio technique: Self-Referential Testing (turning the opponent’s universal claim directly back onto the sentence they just uttered).

During the Warring States period, amid endless debates among Confucians, Mohists, Logicians, and Legalists, a fashionable posture of sweeping skepticism emerged. Certain Daoist relativists and world-weary commentators declared: “All speech under heaven is false, one-sided, and perverse (yi yan wei jin bei 以言为尽悖)!”

Ordinary listeners found this assertion hard to refute—because the moment you opened your mouth to argue against it, the skeptic would smile and say: “See? What you just said is also speech, so what you said is false too.”

Yet the authors of the Mohist Canons (Jing Xia and Jingshuo Xia in the Mozi) demolished this posture in fewer than thirty characters—a textbook counter-kill in the global history of logic:

Canon II (Jing Xia): “To claim that all speech is perverse (bei) is itself perverse; the explanation lies in one’s own speech.” （以言为尽悖，悖，说在其言。）
Explanations II (Jingshuo Xia): “Perverse (bei) means inadmissible. If this person’s speech is admissible, then at least this speech is not perverse, meaning there is speech that is admissible; if this person’s speech is inadmissible, then taking it as hitting the mark certainly fails upon examination.” （悖，不可也。之人之言可，是不悖，则是有可也；之人之言不可，以当必不审。）

Let us translate the Mohists’ razor-sharp classical prose into modern logical steps:

You claim that “All statements are false (yi yan wei jin bei).” Very well—I do not need to hunt for outside counterexamples; I will simply test the sentence you just spoke (shuo zai qi yan):

  • Case 1: Suppose your statement “All statements are false” is itself true (admissible). If it is true, then there exists at least one true statement in the world (namely, your statement), which directly falsifies your claim that “all statements are false” (shi bu bei, ze shi you ke ye).
  • Case 2: Suppose your statement “All statements are false” is itself false (inadmissible). If your own conclusion is false by your own admission, why should anyone believe it (yi dang bi bu shen)?

Whether you pick Case 1 or Case 2, the sentence devours itself the instant it leaves your mouth, like a snake biting off its own tail.

Even better, when anti-intellectuals of the Warring States era declared that “studying and learning are completely useless (xue zhi wu yi),” the Mojing deployed the exact same self-referential scalpel (Jing Xia and Jingshuo Xia):

Canon II (Jing Xia): “That learning is beneficial—the explanation lies in those who denounce it.” （学之益也，说在诽者。）
Explanations II (Jingshuo Xia): “Regarding learning: thinking that others do not know that learning is useless, one therefore informs them of it. Informing them so that they come to know that learning is useless is teaching them. To hold that learning is useless and yet to teach—that is perverse (bei)!” （学也，以为不知学之无益也，故告之也。是使智（知）学之无益也，是教也。以学为无益也，教，悖！）

Look at how lethal the Mohist argument is: why does the critic of education (feizhe) go around lecturing people that “learning is useless”? Because he believes people have not yet learned the truth that “learning is useless,” so he teaches them in order to make them learn it (shi shi zhi xue zhi wu yi ye)—yet the very act of teaching others to learn that proposition is teaching and learning (shi jiao ye)! Preaching that learning is useless while actively teaching people to learn your doctrine is slapping your own face in public (yi xue wei wu yi ye, jiao, bei)! Likewise, against those who proclaim “I oppose all criticism,” Canon II condenses the rebuttal into six characters: “To condemn condemnation is perverse; the explanation lies in not condemning” （非诽者悖，说在弗非）—by criticizing others for criticizing, your own sentence is already an act of criticism.

This Mohist operation—“testing your universal claim against the very act of uttering it”—anticipated Bertrand Russell’s discovery of set-theoretic paradoxes (such as the Barber Paradox) by twenty-two hundred years. It is purpose-built to puncture people in everyday life who use universal negations or absolute relativism to sound profound.

Deep Dive Why Does Universal Self-Reference Trigger System Collapse?—From the Mojing to Russell’s Theory of Types and Tarski’s Hierarchy of Languages

Why do statements like the Mohists’ “All speech is false” or Epimenides’ Liar Paradox (“This statement is false”) pack such destructive power?

At the turn of the twentieth century, British philosopher and mathematician Bertrand Russell used this exact structure to formulate Russell’s Paradox (popularized as the Barber Paradox), which triggered the third foundational crisis in mathematics: In a certain village lives a barber who hangs a sign outside his shop proclaiming an iron rule: “I shave all—and only—those men in the village who do not shave themselves.” On the surface, the rule sounds crystal clear. Until one day, someone asks the barber a Mohist question: “Excuse me, do you shave yourself?”

  • If the barber does not shave himself, then by the definition on his sign, he belongs to the group of “men who do not shave themselves,” which means he must shave himself;
  • If the barber does shave himself, then he belongs to the group of “men who shave themselves,” and by his sign’s rule that he shaves only those who do not shave themselves, he must not shave himself!

Why do the Mohists’ “All speech is perverse,” the ancient Greek Liar Paradox, and Russell’s Barber Paradox all lock up in the same “if true then false; if false then true” oscillation?

Logicians Alfred Tarski and Bertrand Russell ultimately uncovered the underlying theoretical explanation: The Theory of Types and the Confusion of Object Language with Meta-Language.

In a healthy cognitive and linguistic system, language is organized into distinct floors (levels):

  • Floor 1 is the “Object Language”: used to describe concrete objects and events in the world (e.g., “It is raining today,” “The third table in this report has a math error”);
  • Floor 2 is the “Meta-Language”: used to evaluate the truth, falsity, or status of statements on Floor 1 (e.g., “What Alice just said on Floor 1 is true”).

Tarski proved an iron law of logic: Statements on Floor 2 can evaluate statements on Floor 1, but no level of language may bundle itself into the very set of statements it is evaluating (no cross-level self-swallowing)! Whenever someone declares, “All statements in the world are false,” “All statistics are lies,” or “Every organizational process crushes human creativity,” they are standing on Floor 1 trying to detonate a bomb that blows up Floor 1, Floor 2, and the entire building—including the sentence they are speaking right now.

Once you grasp Tarski’s and the Mojing’s principle of linguistic levels, you gain X-ray vision against grandstanding sophistry in everyday life: whenever someone pronounces a sweeping universal negation, they are illicitly hiding themselves outside the set of “all” (pretending to sit on God’s second floor judging humanity’s first floor). All you have to do is politely invite them down the stairs and place their own sentence into the basket they just defined—and their absolutist dogma falls apart on the spot.

3.3 Treating Contradictions as a Fault-Diagnosis Instrument for Your Cognitive System

Combine Zeno’s outward extrapolation with the Mohists’ inward self-reference, and you get a high-powered Assumption Stress-Testing Protocol for daily work, reading, and decision-making.

Whenever you draft a new team rule, encounter an investment maxim, or listen to a consultant pitch a management doctrine, do not just admire its success stories in fair weather—proactively subject it to a two-step reductio diagnostic:

Operation 1: The Mohist “Self-Referential Test” (For Absolutist Slogans)

Whenever you hear a claim containing “all,” “every,” “absolutely,” or “never,” apply the sentence directly to the speaker’s own act of speaking.

  • Example 1: “There are no objective facts in the world; all news reports and data analyses are nothing but ideological propaganda.”
    • Mohist Test: Excuse me, is the sentence you just spoke—“There are no objective facts in the world”—an objective fact, or is it just your ideological propaganda? If it is an objective fact, you have just proved that objective facts exist; if it is merely your propaganda, why should I treat it as universally true?
  • Example 2: A management guru shouts at a corporate seminar: “In the internet age, all past experience is toxic baggage—the only rule is to disrupt every rule!”
    • Mohist Test: If “all past experience is toxic baggage,” isn’t the consulting framework you distilled from your past client cases—and are charging us $20,000 today to learn—also toxic baggage we should throw out immediately?

Operation 2: Zeno’s “Extreme Extrapolation Test” (Finding Where a Rule Breaks Down)

In management and policy design, many rules work well at “normal dosages,” but their authors forget to specify boundary conditions. By following the rule’s internal logic and turning its dial to the extreme, we can see where the system will blow up before launching it.

  • Example (Reductio on Goodhart’s Law): To improve customer service quality, a call center enacts a rule: “100% of a customer-service representative’s bonus is tied to their post-call ‘5-Star Satisfaction Rate’; anyone scoring below 99% loses their bonus.”
    • Zeno-Style Extreme Deduction: Assume this rule is enforced to the letter. What will rational employees do to hit a 100% five-star rate?
      1. When an angry customer calls demanding a refund that company policy strictly forbids, whoever handles that call is guaranteed a 1-star rating. Experienced reps quickly learn to pretend the line dropped and hang up within five seconds, or transfer the call to an unsuspecting rookie;
      2. For customers who can be pacified with giveaways, reps hand out unauthorized cash vouchers like candy just to buy a 5-star click.
    • Diagnosed Crack: Running this 5-minute extreme deduction on Day 1 exposes the flaw in the rule: it conflates “customer satisfaction with the rep’s politeness” and “customer anger at company return policies.” Unless those two dimensions are separated (e.g., exempting negative ratings caused by enforcing mandatory company rules upon supervisor review), the policy will drive out honest reps within three months.

Deep Dive Why Do Concepts Start “Leaking” the Longer We Use Them?—Lakatosian Research Programmes and Conceptual Stretching

In Section 3.3 we called paradoxes and reductio ad absurdum a “fault-diagnosis instrument” for conceptual systems. But why do concepts and rules crafted by smart predecessors—rules that worked seamlessly when first introduced—develop cracks and generate absurdities over time?

Philosopher of science Imre Lakatos’s Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes and political scientist Giovanni Sartori’s theory of Conceptual Stretching explain the three inevitable mechanisms of conceptual aging:

  1. The “Initial Anchor Domain” vs. Cross-Boundary Conceptual Stretching
    • Every concept or rule is originally distilled from a specific, finite pool of empirical cases (its initial anchor domain). Newtonian mechanics was distilled within the domain of macroscopic, low-velocity, weak-gravity phenomena; a startup’s rule of “flat hierarchy with zero approval workflows” was distilled within the domain of ten co-founders sitting in a single garage who know each other’s every move.
    • As practical scope expands, people unconsciously carry the exact same word or rule into radically different domains (relativistic subatomic particles, or a 500-person multi-office enterprise)—what Sartori calls Conceptual Stretching. To cover new phenomena, the extension of the concept is stretched wider and wider while its defining intension and enabling conditions are diluted until they are full of holes. Give it a gentle push toward the boundary with Zeno’s reductio, and its hidden premise cracks wide open.
  2. The Philosophical Lesson of Gödel’s Incompleteness Theorems: The Tension Between Consistency and Completeness
    • In 1931, logician Kurt Gödel proved his earth-shaking Incompleteness Theorems: in any formal axiomatic system rich enough to contain basic arithmetic, as long as the system is consistent (free of contradiction), it is necessarily incomplete—meaning there will always exist true statements that cannot be proved from within that system’s own axioms, and the system’s consistency cannot be proved solely from within itself without ascending to a higher meta-system.
    • While Gödel’s theorem is a strict mathematical result that should not be sloppily equated with everyday clichés, it established a permanent landmark in human epistemology: No single finite rulebook or conceptual system can be simultaneously 100% free of edge-case blind spots and 100% exhaustive of all reality.
    • When you discover that a management policy, KPI rubric, or personal creed produces an absurd “following the rule to the letter ruins the goal” paradox at the margins, do not panic—your system has simply hit its incompleteness boundary. The right move, just as set theorists did after Russell’s Paradox and physicists did after the ultraviolet catastrophe of blackbody radiation, is to use that paradox as a springboard to upgrade your system’s axioms (Chapter 10’s negation of the negation).

Common Pitfalls: Reductio Is for Refining Cognition, Not for Being a Contrarian Troll

When using reductio ad absurdum in daily communication, there is a major social and cognitive trap: turning a diagnostic instrument into a contrarian weapon for winning petty arguments.

We have all met this person online or in meetings: a wife casually remarks, “You always leave your dirty socks on the floor,” and her husband immediately launches a formal reductio: “Last Wednesday I put them in the hamper! Your universal claim that I always leave them on the floor violates factual logic!” Or a colleague suggests, “We should listen more closely to frontline user feedback,” and a contrarian snaps back: “If we listened 100% to users, when Henry Ford asked people what they wanted, they would have said a faster horse—should we go breed horses?”

That is not dialectical thinking; that is the Straw Man Fallacy—deliberately distorting a reasonable, context-bounded everyday suggestion into a caricatured extreme, and then knocking down that fake target to score cheap rhetorical points.

How can you tell whether you are practicing genuine reductio diagnosis or acting like a contrarian troll? Ask yourself one question: After pointing out the crack in the other person’s statement, did you help patch the crack by specifying the boundary conditions under which their point holds?

  • The Contrarian Troll’s Move: Prove that “listening blindly to users leads to breeding faster horses” →\rightarrow Conclusion: “Therefore listening to users is stupid; ignore them.” (Destroys dialogue, zero progress.)
  • The Dialectical Thinker’s Move: Use extreme deduction to show that “copying users’ literal feature requests (a faster horse) stifles innovation, whereas ignoring users’ underlying pain points (getting to their destination faster) detaches us from the market” →\rightarrow Refined Conclusion: “When collecting user feedback, we should log their frictions and pain points rather than blindly copying their proposed engineering solutions.” (Patches the crack, advances cognition.)

Thinking Exercises

Case Analysis: The “Anti-Process Rebellion” in a Creative Agency

A fast-growing creative video agency and MCN studio expanded from 15 people to 80 people in one year. Plagued by lost raw footage, missed client revision notes, and chaotic expense reimbursements, the newly hired Operations Director introduced a Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) requiring all directors to fill out a Storyboard Approval Form and a Line-Item Budget Sheet before shooting.

At the rollout meeting, the agency’s top-grossing creative director, Zhang, stood up and delivered a speech that drew thunderous applause from the creative floor: “The lifeblood of the creative industry is freedom and inspiration! Every process, form, and approval workflow is by nature a machine for killing creativity. Back when there were 15 of us, we had zero forms and produced three viral hits with ten million views! Forcing us to fill out bureaucratic boxes is turning artists into assembly-line robots!”

If you are the Operations Director facing Zhang’s emotionally charged universal claim, pulling rank to force compliance will trigger a mutiny among your best creatives, yet backing down leaves an 80-person company bleeding cash from preventable blunders every day. Use Zeno’s and the Mohists’ tools from this chapter to expose the cracks in Zhang’s proposition and design a revised institutional solution.

Reference Analysis Pathway (Not the only solution; designed to demonstrate reductio unpacking and rule patching):

  1. Step 1: Internal Diagnosis—Use Zeno’s Extreme Extrapolation and Mohist Self-Reference to Locate the Cracks in Zhang’s Premise
    • Zhang’s core premise is: “Every process and rule kills creativity, and the zero-rule model of a 15-person shop works for an 80-person company.”
    • Mohist Self-Referential Test: When Zhang directs a shoot himself, does he require his cameramen to show up on time? Does he require his video editors to organize raw footage using a consistent folder-naming convention? If his assistant editor announced tomorrow, “Folder-naming rules crush my editing inspiration, so I dumped 500 video clips randomly across five hard drives,” would Zhang accept that? Clearly, Zhang does not oppose all rules—his own creative work relies on foundational coordination rules.
    • Zeno-Style Extreme Extrapolation: Push “80 people operating with zero forms and purely verbal requests” to its logical conclusion—when 30 shoots run concurrently, finance has no idea which vendor deposit to wire first, the gear room has no idea who took the cinema lenses, and a client’s verbal script tweak skips legal review, resulting in a regulatory violation that gets the flagship account banned and fined $70,000. When the account is banned and payroll bounces, where is “creative freedom”?
  2. Step 2: Public Dialogue—Use Gentle Reductio Questions to Let the Team See the Boundary Conditions (Present Consequences Without Humiliating Anyone)
    • In the meeting, do not debate the abstract word “process.” Instead, anchor your questions in two painful incidents from the previous month: > “Zhang’s point is spot-on about one thing: nobody here wants to turn directors into form-filling clerks. But let’s play ‘zero rules’ all the way out. Last Wednesday, after your team wrapped that car commercial, because the freelance assistant left without logging the hard-drive directory, post-production spent two full days hunting for the 4K master footage and nearly triggered a $30,000 breach-of-contract penalty. And last month, Team B’s viral video got taken down two hours after launch because nobody ran the script past the advertising-law banned-word checklist—wiping out three all-nighters of brilliant creative work. > Let me ask everyone: what kills your creative work more—spending 10 minutes checking a pre-flight safety list, or watching a masterpiece you poured three sleepless nights into get deleted permanently over an avoidable compliance slip?”
  3. Step 3: Patch the Crack—Separate “Administrative Certainty” from “Creative Autonomy”
    • The crack is now clear: the original SOP provoked rebellion because it lumped creative content itself (how to frame shots, how to write jokes) together with baseline logistical guardrails (how money is wired, where footage is backed up, whether banned words are screened).
    • Revised Solution:
      1. Abolish Layperson Storyboard Approvals Completely: Operations and administration will never judge or approve a director’s creative script, humor, or visual style (preserving 100% creative autonomy);
      2. Retain Only a 3-Item “Crash-Prevention Pre-Flight Checklist” (Taking Under 10 Minutes on Mobile): ① Gear & budget reservation ticket (ensuring funds and cameras are ready); ② A one-page ad-law banned-word self-check (ensuring the video stays online); ③ Cloud backup path for raw dailies (ensuring creative work is never lost). By turning bloated paperwork into a 3-click mobile pre-flight check, rules stop acting like a straitjacket and become a safety harness protecting the artists’ work.

Personal Exercises

  1. Self-Referential Test on Everyday Clichés: Examine two absolutist sayings you or people around you often repeat: “I never take advice from anyone,” or “In this society, honest people always finish last.” Test them with the Mohist method (shuo zai qi yan): if a close friend earnestly advises you, “Whatever you do, never take advice from anyone,” do you follow their advice or not? And if “honest people always finish last,” when two honest people partner up in business, who takes advantage of whom?
  2. Rule Stress-Testing: Pick an existing rule in your team (or a personal rule you set for yourself). Extrapolate its literal logic three steps toward the extreme: under what specific scenario would following this rule to the letter actively sabotage the core purpose it was meant to serve? Write a one-sentence boundary clause that seals that loophole.
