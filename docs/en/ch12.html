Part V

Chapter 12 The Principal Aspect of a Contradiction: Assessing Power Asymmetry and Dominant Momentum

How to Engineer a Reversal in an Asymmetric Game

In Chapter 11, we learned how to isolate the single Principal Contradiction out of five or six competing problems.

Now, when you hold that single principal contradiction in your hands and prepare to act on it, you discover two opposing forces locked in a tug-of-war inside it—for example, buyer versus seller in a commercial negotiation; an incumbent giant versus an upstart challenger in market competition; or “your new resolve to change” versus “decades of entrenched habit inertia” in personal growth.

In the classical dialectical toolkit, the companion instrument right next to “Principal Contradiction” is the Principal Aspect of a Contradiction: inside any single contradiction, the two contending sides are almost never balanced at an exact 50–50 symmetry; one side necessarily holds the dominant, governing position (the Principal Aspect), while the other sits in a subordinate, secondary position (the Secondary Aspect). The current nature of the situation—and your correct strategic posture—is determined by whichever side currently holds the dominant position.

If Chapter 11 taught you how to pick the right battlefield, Chapter 12 teaches you how to read the balance of power and pick the right playbook.

12.1 Power Asymmetry: Sun Tzu’s “Invincibility Lies in Oneself; Vulnerability Lies in the Enemy” and Distinct Playbooks for Strong vs. Weak Positions

In real-world strategic interactions, the dumbest mistake is playing the exact same hand regardless of whether you hold the upper or lower hand—running a tiny five-person shop with $10,000 in working capital yet imitating a cash-rich industry giant’s “burn-cash-to-subsidize-market-share” blitz; or holding the sole indispensable patent at a negotiation table yet letting a procurement officer bluff you into slashing your price by half because you failed to audit your leverage.

Two thousand five hundred years ago, the Art of War (Sunzi Bingfa, Chapter 4: Tactical Dispositions / Xing Pian) laid down the iron law of asymmetric action with icy arithmetic precision:

“In ancient times, those skilled in warfare first made themselves invincible, and then awaited the moment when the enemy could be conquered. Invincibility lies in oneself; conquerability lies in the enemy. Therefore, the skilled warrior can make himself invincible, but cannot force the enemy to be certainly vulnerable. Hence it is said: Victory can be discerned, but cannot be forced. Invincibility is a matter of defense; vulnerability is a matter of attack. Defense indicates insufficient strength; attack indicates abundant strength.”（昔之善战者，先为不可胜，以待敌之可胜。不可胜在己，可胜在敌。故善战者，能为不可胜，不能使敌之必可胜。故曰：胜可知，而不可为。不可胜者，守也；可胜者，攻也。守则不足，攻则有余。）

And in Chapter 3 (Attack by Stratagem / Mougong Pian), Sun Tzu provided an explicit calibration scale for switching tactics according to the ratio of forces:

“Therefore the art of employing troops is this: when ten to the enemy’s one, surround him; when five to one, attack him; when two to one, divide him; when equally matched, be capable of engaging him; when fewer in number, be capable of withdrawing; when decidedly inferior, be capable of avoiding battle. For if a small force stubbornly fights a pitched battle, it becomes the captive of the larger force.”（故用兵之法，十则围之，五则攻之，倍则分之，敌则能战之，少则能逃之，不若则能避之。故小敌之坚，大敌之擒也。）

Read those final eight characters closely: “For if a small force stubbornly fights a pitched battle, it becomes the captive of the larger force”（故小敌之坚，大敌之擒也）!

When the weaker side ignores a massive disparity in strength and trades blows head-on with the dominant side on the main battlefield (xiao di zhi jian), that is not courage—it is volunteering to become the prisoner of the stronger adversary (da di zhi qin ye)!

Translating Sun Tzu’s assessment of principal and secondary aspects into modern negotiation theory and personal/corporate strategy means that before playing a single card, we must first audit who currently occupies the dominant aspect (whose leverage is harder):

1. When You Occupy the Secondary / Weaker Aspect (“Defense Indicates Insufficient Strength; First Make Yourself Invincible”)

When you are a newcomer entering an industry dominated by entrenched giants, or when your hand at a workplace negotiation table is weak:

  • Absolute Taboo: Engaging in a war of attrition on the opponent’s home turf where they enjoy overwhelming scale (e.g., a small independent online shop trying to beat Amazon or Pinduoduo on lowest price for standardized commodity goods).
  • Correct Strategy (Locally Inverting the Principal and Secondary Aspects): Although the giant holds the dominant aspect globally, its massive overhead makes it structurally unwilling to bend down and service “niche segments that are too small in volume and require high-touch customization.” Your playbook is two-pronged: first, keep fixed costs ultra-low and guard your survival cash-flow floor (invincibility lies in oneself); second, carve out a razor-thin vertical niche that the giant disdains, where your 5-person team can concentrate 100% of its firepower to achieve a 10×10\times local superiority—flipping the secondary aspect into the principal aspect inside that micro-theater until you have built enough reserves to expand.

2. When You Occupy the Principal / Dominant Aspect (“Attack Indicates Abundant Strength, Yet Beware the Arrogance of Dominance”)

When you hold the commanding upper hand in a partnership, negotiation, or market, your task is to convert temporary momentum into institutional standards or durable switching-cost moats, while staying hyper-vigilant against the “momentum reversal” we will examine in Section 12.2.

3. Auditing the Principal Aspect at the Negotiation Table: Whose BATNA Is Harder?

The Harvard Negotiation Project introduced a foundational concept in negotiation science: BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)—namely, “If today’s negotiation walks away without a deal, what is the best concrete fallback option I have in hand?” In any salary negotiation, commercial procurement deal, or partnership dispute, nobody becomes the “Principal Aspect of the Contradiction” by pounding the table louder; the side with the stronger walk-away endurance (BATNA) is the Principal Aspect.

  • If you walk into your boss’s office to demand a raise with zero external job offers in hand and a $3,000 mortgage payment due next month (your BATNA is terrible), while fifty qualified applicants on the job market can replace your role tomorrow (your boss’s BATNA is strong)—your boss is the absolute Principal Aspect of this relationship. Threatening to resign as a bluff is guaranteed suicide (“a small force stubbornly fighting becomes the captive of the larger force”).
  • Conversely, if you already hold a signed offer letter from a competitor paying 30% more, and the company’s three largest client accounts rely personally on your technical expertise—the principal and secondary aspects have already flipped. You don’t need to raise your voice by a single decibel; you simply lay the market compensation benchmarks calmly on the desk, and the governing initiative rests in your hands.

Why does “a small force that stubbornly fights a giant head-on become its captive”? In organizational sociology, Jeffrey Pfeffer and Gerald Salancik’s Resource Dependence Theory (1978) shows that power in any relationship equals Criticality of Resource ×\times Scarcity (Irreplaceability). On a broad commodity battlefield, a startup is 100% replaceable while the incumbent holds all scale advantages; only by retreating into a narrow vertical niche where your proprietary solution is irreplaceable to a specific customer segment do you invert the resource-dependence ratio and become the dominant aspect locally.

12.2 Dynamic Reversal of Momentum: When the Dominant Party Mistakes a Flowing Advantage for Permanent Real Estate

Why must we keep our eyes glued to the “Principal Aspect of a Contradiction” in Chapter 12? Because just like the Principal Contradiction, the principal and secondary aspects inside a contradiction are never welded in place—their balance of power waxes, wanes, and trades places continuously over time.

In reality, why do so many players who once held overwhelming dominance (industry leaders, indispensable senior employees, the more-loved partner in a relationship) eventually fall from grace? Because they commit a fatal cognitive error: mistaking a dominant position maintained by dynamic flow for permanent real estate that can never depreciate.

Let us trace a three-stage “reversal of principal and secondary aspects” that plays out every day in business and the workplace:

  • Stage 1 (The Supplier / Subordinate Occupies the Weak Secondary Aspect): A newly founded precision-component supplier (or a newly hired junior engineer), eager to win orders from a major automaker, adopts a humble posture—answering calls at midnight and modifying prototypes free of charge. At this point, the automaker’s procurement department holds absolute dominance (the Principal Aspect), thinking, “Without our orders, your little shop would starve.” So the automaker squeezes prices, stretches payment terms, and lets its own internal engineering team stop studying low-level component工艺, outsourcing all the hard technical dirty work to the small supplier.
  • Stage 2 (Silent Underground Shift in Capabilities): Three years pass. Through grinding away at the hardest engineering tolerances every day, the small supplier lifts core yield rates from 60% to 99%, secures over a dozen non-circumventable process patents, and signs five major overseas automakers—reducing its revenue dependence on the original automaker below 15%. Meanwhile, the original automaker’s internal engineers, having acted as hands-off middlemen for three years, have completely lost the ability to calibrate the component independently.
  • Stage 3 (Complete Reversal of Principal and Secondary Aspects): In Year 4, when the automaker’s flagship new vehicle urgently needs a ramp-up of that core component and the procurement director struts in with the same arrogant posture from three years ago to demand a price cut, he discovers to his shock that the table has turned 180 degrees: now the automaker cannot build its cars without this supplier, whereas if the automaker walks away, overseas clients will snap up the supplier’s freed capacity within twenty-four hours. The governing and subordinate positions have traded places silently beneath the surface.

Therefore, dialectical momentum assessment requires keeping a定期 “Dynamic Power Balance Sheet”:

  • If you currently occupy the dominant aspect, ask yourself every six months: “Is the core moat supporting my dominant seat today getting thicker, or is it being hollowed out by my own complacency and outsourcing of hard work?”
  • If you currently occupy the weaker aspect, skip emotional self-pity and ask: “Over the next twelve months, which concrete daily action am I taking that quietly lowers my dependence on the other party (thickening my BATNA) while raising the cost they would pay to replace me?”

Why do industry giants so often die from “excellent management”? In The Innovator’s Dilemma (1997), Clayton Christensen showed that well-run incumbents are prisoners of their own Resource Allocation Processes: because middle managers’ bonuses and promotions are tied to short-term gross margins and existing flagship customers, any nascent disruptive technology (which always starts out small, low-margin, and rough around the edges) is automatically starved of talent and budget by the company’s own rational ROI filters.

Common Pitfall: Hiding Behind “Both Sides Are Evenly Matched and Each Has Its Strengths” to Excuse Analytical Laziness

In competitive analysis or strategic assessment, the most worthless boilerplate report reads: “Company A has brand strength, while Company B has technical strength; Plan A has lower risk, while Plan B has higher upside—both sides have their own merits and are evenly matched.”

Remember: in the real physical and commercial world, an exact 50–50 tie is as rare and fleeting as balancing a coin on its edge. Nine times out of ten, declaring “both sides have their merits and are evenly matched” simply means the analyst was too lazy to calculate the weights: at this specific stage of the market, does “brand recognition” drive 70% of customer purchasing decisions, or does “technical parameter superiority” drive 70%?

A comparison without weighted assessment is just a lifeless bullet list.

Thinking Exercises

Case Analysis: Life-or-Death Choice When a Six-Person Startup Collides Head-On with an Industry Titan

The Dilemma: Six-person B2B software startup “CloudPulse” built a general-purpose project management tool. Just as they hit 50 paying clients, an industry giant with 3,000 engineers bundled a nearly identical general project-management module into its enterprise suite for free. CloudPulse’s co-founders split: Co-founder A wanted to raise emergency debt and match the giant’s 20 new features head-on; Co-founder B wanted to slash prices to zero.

  • Reference Analytical Approach:
    1. Audit the Principal Aspect & Resource Dependence Ratio (Ch. 12.1): On the broad “general project management” battlefield, the giant holds 100% of the scale, brand, and distribution dominance—fighting head-on on feature count or free pricing guarantees “a small force becoming the giant’s captive.”
    2. Invert the Local Dominance Ratio by Narrowing the Battlefield (Ch. 12.3): Reviewing their 50 paying clients revealed that 14 were mid-sized medical-device manufacturers who paid gladly because CloudPulse happened to support FDA/NMPA ISO-13485 batch-traceability audit logs—a regulatory niche too specialized and legally complex for the giant’s generic free tool to touch.
    3. Strategic Pivot: Immediately abandon general-purpose marketing, rename the product as a dedicated Medical-Device R&D Compliance & Traceability Workspace, and concentrate all 6 engineers on building deep regulatory templates—converting their position from a replaceable minnow in a 10-billion ocean into the irreplaceable dominant leader of a high-margin vertical trench.
