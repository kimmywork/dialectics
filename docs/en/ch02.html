Part I

Chapter 2 A Lesson from Kant and the Pre-Qin School of Names: Some Debates Arise Because the Question Crossed the Line

In Chapter 1, we learned to distinguish contradictions, trade-offs, dilemmas, and paradoxes. Yet in real life, you will also run into an even stranger breed of argument:

The affirmative side presents an argument that is logical, well-structured, and airtight; the negative side presents a counterargument that is equally rigorous and watertight. The two camps have debated for decades—or millennia—without either side convincing the other, and whichever side you listen to sounds completely right.

When this happens, most people assume “we just don’t have enough evidence yet” and double down on gathering ammunition to crush the opponent. Yet the eighteenth-century German philosopher Immanuel Kant and the logicians and Daoists of pre-Qin China offered a radically different diagnosis: When two opposing sides can both justify themselves with equal logical perfection, what is broken is usually not the answer, but the question itself.

2.1 Kant’s Antinomies: Why Can Both Sides Make a Watertight Case?

In 1781, in the “Transcendental Dialectic” section of his Critique of Pure Reason, Immanuel Kant laid out four pairs of parallel proofs that shook the philosophical world, calling them the “Antinomies of Pure Reason.”

What is an antinomy? It is a deadlock over an ultimate question in which both the Thesis (pro) and the Antithesis (con) can prove their own truth—and the opponent’s falsity—using impeccable formal logic.

We do not need to memorize all four philosophical pairs; let us look at the most intuitive First Antinomy: “Does the universe have a beginning in time and a boundary in space?”

  • Proof of the Thesis (The universe has a beginning in time and a boundary in space): Suppose the universe had no beginning in time. Then, up to this very moment (“today”), an infinite series of successive states of things must have already elapsed. By definition, however, an “infinite” series can never be completed through successive synthesis. If the past series of time could never be completed, time could never have arrived at “today.” Since “today” has undeniably arrived, past time must be finite—and therefore the universe must have had a beginning.
  • Proof of the Antithesis (The universe has no beginning and no boundary): Suppose the universe had a beginning in time. Then prior to that beginning, there must have been an “empty time” in which nothing existed. In an empty time, however, no moment possesses any distinguishing condition over any other moment to explain why a universe should suddenly pop into existence at that instant rather than another. Therefore, the universe could not have begun in empty time; it must be infinite.

Notice that the Thesis uses the reductio ad absurdum method we will study in Chapter 3, and the Antithesis uses the exact same reductio method. Neither side violates formal logic, yet their conclusions are diametrically opposed. Kant’s other three antinomies (“Are all composite substances made of indivisible simple particles, or is matter infinitely divisible?”; “Does free will exist, or is everything determined by natural causality?”; “Does an absolutely necessary Being exist, or is everything contingent?”) exhibit the exact same structure.

Why does reason lock up like this?

Tracing this intellectual history in Dialectical Thinking: Zeno, Socrates, Kant, Marx (2015), Finnish philosopher Tommi Juhani Hanhijärvi points out that such paradoxes arise not because our reason is too weak to find an answer, but precisely because our deductive power is so strong that, once unmoored from empirical facts, it can spin out two equally coherent yet mutually colliding chains of inference.

Kant’s diagnosis was surgical: human cognitive categories (time, space, causality) are designed to process concrete phenomena within the empirical world (such as measuring the dimensions of a room or explaining why water boils at 100∘C100^\circ\text{C}). The moment we try to apply those same yardsticks to measure the totality of the entire cosmos or an unconditioned whole beyond all possible experience, our reason steps out of bounds.

It is as if you are holding a scale built to weigh fish caught from the sea: as long as a fish is hauled onto the deck, your scale can tell you whether it weighs three pounds or five. But if someone asks you to “put the entire ocean itself into the basket and weigh it,” your scale breaks on the spot. Kant’s first great dialectical lesson for us is this: Whenever two opposing positions are equally self-consistent and locked in an endless loop, pause and inspect the question—has the framing of the question itself already crossed beyond the boundary of what empirical facts can test?

2.2 Parallel Discoveries in Pre-Qin China: Hui Shi’s “Greatest Has Nothing Beyond,” Zhuangzi’s “Simultaneous Birth and Death,” and Gongsun Long’s “Separating Hard and White”

Remarkably, two thousand years before Kant penned the Critique of Pure Reason, the Disputers (Bianzhe 辩者, the School of Names) and Daoists of Warring States China—working in complete isolation from Greek and European philosophy—ran into virtually the exact same cognitive boundary puzzles.

Chapter 33 of the Zhuangzi (“All Under Heaven” / Tianxia) records the “Ten Theses” of Hui Shi (Zhuangzi’s closest friend and favorite sparring partner), opening with a definition of the макро- and micro-limits of space:

“That which is greatest has nothing beyond it—it is called the Great One (Da Yi); that which is smallest has nothing within it—it is called the Small One (Xiao Yi).”
（至大无外，谓之大一；至小无内，谓之小一。）

A few lines later, the Tianxia chapter records another proposition fiercely debated by the logicians of the era:

“Take a foot-long stick and halve it each day; in ten thousand generations it will never be exhausted.”
（一尺之棰，日取其半，万世不竭。）

Place these two classical Chinese propositions side by side with Kant’s Second Antinomy:

  • Hui Shi’s “Smallest One with nothing within it” points to a minimal, indivisible elementary unit with no internal structure (corresponding to Kant’s Thesis: composite things consist of indivisible simple parts);
  • Meanwhile, “a foot-long stick halved each day will never be exhausted in ten thousand generations” captures the infinite divisibility of spatial magnitude in mathematical logic (corresponding to Kant’s Antithesis: nothing simple exists, and everything is infinitely divisible).

Why does a wooden stick in the physical workshop turn into sawdust after a dozen chops (the physical/empirical “Small One”), whereas in mathematical thought it can be divided by two forever (the conceptual “never exhausted in ten thousand generations”)? Because one statement operates in the domain of physical manipulation, while the other operates in the domain of pure conceptual iteration. When two people standing in different domains argue over whether “the stick can be chopped to the end,” the argument can never end.

The exact same dimensional misalignment drives the famous debate over “Separating Hard and White” (Jian Bai Zhi Bian 坚白之辩). In his essay Discourse on Hard and White (Jianbailun), Gongsun Long uses a stone that is both hard (jian) and white (bai):

“Looking at the stone, one does not get its hardness, but gets its whiteness—so there is no hardness [in sight]; touching the stone, one does not get its whiteness, but gets its hardness—so there is no whiteness [in touch].”
（视不得其所坚，而得其所白者，无坚也；拊不得其所白，而得其所坚者，无白也。）

Gongsun Long argues: when you look at the stone with your eyes, you register only “white,” not “hard”; when you close your eyes and feel the stone with your hand, you register only “hard,” not “white.” Therefore, “hardness” and “whiteness” are mutually separated in perception and cannot be grasped together (li jian bai 离坚白).

The Mohist School (Mojing), by contrast, pushed back forcefully with the doctrine of “Hard and White Mutually Fill Each Other” (jian bai xiang ying 坚白相盈): hardness and whiteness clearly co-inhabit the exact same physical volume of stone—how could anyone claim they are separate?

Who was right? Both sides spoke the truth within their respective reference frames: Gongsun Long was dissecting the separation of human sensory channels (visual processing and tactile processing run along independent neural pathways), whereas the Mohists were describing the unity of objective physical properties (a single material object possesses both attributes simultaneously). The fact that two civilizations two millennia apart collided with the exact same antinomies proves that these tensions are not word games invented by eccentric philosophers, but structural tremors triggered whenever human thought crosses the border between “sensory channel and physical entity” or “finite experience and infinite extrapolation.”

★ 2.3 Real-World Application: When an Argument Hits a Dead Loop, Audit the Question’s Presuppositions First

You might ask: in daily life we rarely debate whether the cosmos had a beginning or whether hardness and whiteness are separate—how does Kant and Hui Shi’s “boundary awareness” help me at work or at home?

Enormously. Because in office meetings, family fights, and personal overthinking, countless exhausting deadlocks that look like disputes over facts are actually Kantian boundary-crossing questions—questions that smuggle in unfalsifiable universal presuppositions, or twist two incompatible yardsticks together.

Whenever you find a debate where both sides can cite endless examples and never reach a conclusion, run this Three-Step Frame Audit:

Step 1: Spot “Boundary-Crossing Universal Words” (Always, In Essence, At Bottom, Unconditionally)

In the empirical world, we can only verify who did what under what specific timeframe and conditions. The moment a question contains words like “in essence,” “at bottom,” “always,” or “unconditionally,” it has crossed the border into an undecidable metaphysical question.

  • Workplace Example: Two executives spend an hour arguing: “Are Gen-Z employees in essence driven by self-actualization, or do they only care about money?” Camp A can name ten Gen-Z engineers who worked nights for a mission; Camp B can name ten who quit over a $50 bonus difference. The debate is futile because “Gen-Z” is a statistical demographic label covering tens of millions of diverse individuals—there is no singular “essence.” Pull the question back inside empirical boundaries: “Given our company’s current compensation (at the industry median) and promotion track, what were the top three concrete reasons cited by the Gen-Z engineers who resigned over the past six months?” Instantly, a circular philosophical debate turns into a verifiable exit-interview audit.

Step 2: Separate Two Entangled “Measurement Dimensions”

Just as Gongsun Long measured the stone by “sensory channels” while the Mohists measured it by “physical entity,” everyday disputes often erupt because two people are measuring the same object with yardsticks from different dimensions.

  • Public & Business Example: “Is this new drug (or new technology) safe or dangerous?” Proponents cite Phase III clinical trials showing severe adverse events below 1 in 10,000 (population-level statistical probability); opponents circulate a verified video of a patient hospitalized with a life-threatening allergic reaction after taking the pill (individual-level extreme outcome). As long as the two sides fail to separate “population base-rate risk” from “individual tail-risk tragedy,” they can shout past each other online for ten years.

Step 3: Convert “Ultimate定性 (Essentialist Judgments)” into Kantian “Action Compasses”

After proving that pure reason cannot demonstrate whether absolute freedom or ultimate perfection exists as a finished object, Kant did not tell us to give up. Instead, he proposed a brilliant move: downgrade these boundary-crossing concepts from Constitutive Claims (assertions about finished facts) to Regulative Principles (compasses that guide ongoing action).

  • In plain English: you do not need to waste your life arguing over “whether a 100% fair company exists in the world” or “whether a human being can be 100% objective” (which can never be proved empirically and only breeds cynicism or delusion). Instead, treat “greater fairness and greater objectivity” as the North Star overhead. The North Star is not a vacation resort you can walk to and check into, but by sighting along it, you can audit whether the performance-review rubric you revised today is measurably fairer than last month’s version.

Why do we fall into flawed question traps so easily? Linguist George Lakoff showed in Don’t Think of an Elephant! (2004) that every question embeds an unspoken presupposition (“Why has our team lost its fighting spirit?” smuggles in the unverified premise that morale caused the slump). Paired with Tversky and Kahneman’s Framing Effect (1981)—where framing identical outcomes as “200 saved” vs. “400 die” flips choices by 50 percentage points—relying on the questioner’s frame before auditing its boundaries guarantees cognitive capture.

Common Pitfalls: Neither Force an Answer to a Boundary-Crossing Question, Nor Use “That’s Philosophical” to Evade Hard Facts

When applying this chapter’s boundary awareness, guard against erring on either side:

  • Mistake 1: Answering a boundary-crossing question as if it were a factual question. When your partner asks, “If I grow old, lose my looks, become paralyzed, and turn terribly grumpy thirty years from now, will you still love me exactly as you do today?” or your boss asks, “Can you guarantee this new plan will encounter zero hiccups over the next three years?”—if you try to prove empirically “I guarantee 100% yes/no,” you walk straight into an antinomy trap. Because infinite future variables lie beyond anyone’s empirical control, the harder you swear an oath, the easier it is for the other person to invent a counterfactual scenario to doubt you.
  • Mistake 2: Dodging a verifiable factual question by calling it an “undecidable philosophical question.” Suppose an engineering project overruns its budget by $3 million, and when held accountable, the project director strikes a contemplative philosophical pose: “The relationship between budget estimates and actual expenditures, much like Kant’s antinomies, is caught between plan and flux—it is impossible to say whose responsibility it really is.” Immediately invoke Chapter 1’s Han Feizi floor: a $3 million cost overrun is a concrete accounting and project-management fact where every dollar has an invoice and a signature. Philosophical boundaries must never be used as a smokescreen to evade an audit.

Thinking Exercises

Case Analysis: The Circular Couples’ Fight Over “Do You Really Love Me Unconditionally?”

The Dilemma: Every few weeks, Xiaolin and his partner spiral into the same midnight argument. When his partner asks to check his phone messages or demands he cancel a weekend study session, refusing triggers the accusation: “If you truly loved me, you wouldn’t hide anything or put work ahead of me—true love is 100% unconditional!” If he complies to prove his love, resentment builds until the next demand erupts.

  • Reference Analytical Approach:
    1. Apply Zeno’s Reductio to Test the Absolute Premise (Ch. 3.1): Gently push “True love means zero personal boundaries” to its boundary limit (T→100%T \to 100\%): if two people surrender 100% of independent time, friendships, and mental space, both collapse into anxious surveillance and lose the independent vitality that attracted them in the first place.
    2. Spot the Self-Referential Trap (Ch. 3.2): The test “If you don’t obey my demand right now, you don’t love me” is a self-sealing circular trap that equates healthy boundary calibration with betrayal.
    3. Domesticating the Concept into Concrete Protocols (Ch. 3.3): Retire the unmeasurable label “unconditional love” and negotiate two concrete behavioral agreements: High-Certainty Connection Rituals (phones off during a dedicated 90-minute dinner every evening and Sunday date afternoons) paired with Protected Autonomous Windows (Saturday morning professional study blocks with zero guilt-tripping).
