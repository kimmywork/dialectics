Part IV

Chapter 10 The Negation of the Negation: How to Upgrade Without Destroying Past Gains

How to Execute "Abolish, Preserve, Elevate" Asset Migration

We now arrive at the final chapter of our temporal trilogy—and the concept most frequently misunderstood and vulgarized across the entire dialectical tradition: the Negation of the Negation and Sublation (Aufheben).

In everyday life and organizational reform, when an old way of doing things hits a wall and change becomes unavoidable (negating the old model), people routinely fall into one of two opposite follies:

  • The first is “Smash Everything and Start from Zero (Total Nihilism)”: A new leader takes office, declares every institution built by their predecessor, every customer relationship cultivated by the veteran team, and every bit of brand equity in legacy products worthless, and bulldozes the lot—running out of working capital before the new edifice is even half built.
  • The second is “Same Old Wine in a New Bottle (Walking in Circles)”: Shouting slogans of radical reinvention, churning the organization for months, yet merely slapping a trendy new buzzword onto the exact same broken habits and ending up right back at square one.

How can we break free from the shackles of an outdated form without throwing away the core nutrients accumulated inside it, thereby achieving a genuine upgrade at a higher structural level?

In this chapter, we invite Hegel, Engels, the pre-Qin philosopher Xunzi, and Theodor Adorno of the Frankfurt School to unpack the craft of spiral upgrading.

10.1 Hegel’s Aufheben (Sublation) and Fichte: Clearing Up a Two-Hundred-Year Historical Misunderstanding About “Thesis–Antithesis–Synthesis”

Before we dive into operational steps, we need to clear Hegel’s name of a historical misunderstanding that has circulated for two hundred years.

Whenever Hegel’s dialectics is mentioned, nine out of ten people immediately recite three words: “Thesis–Antithesis–Synthesis.”

Yet if you read through Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit and Science of Logic, you will be surprised to discover that Hegel himself almost never used the triad “Thesis, Antithesis, Synthesis” to build his system! The true inventor of that three-step formula was the slightly earlier German philosopher Johann Gottlieb Fichte (along with Schelling), and it was later popularized and pasted onto Hegel by Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus in his popular lectures on the history of philosophy.

Why did Hegel dislike the “Thesis–Antithesis–Synthesis” formula? Because, as he explicitly warned in the Preface to the Phenomenology of Spirit, once thinking is reduced to a mechanical template of “first posit a thesis, then fetch an external antithesis, and finally paste them together into a synthesis,” dialectics degenerates into a lifeless skeleton key slapped indiscriminately onto everything (what we will critique in Section 14.8 as “Mechanical Thesis–Antithesis–Synthesis”).

The core triad Hegel actually used is Affirmation — Negation — Negation of the Negation, and the engine driving this movement is a remarkable German verb: Aufheben (translated into English philosophy as Sublation, and into Chinese by a stroke of genius as 扬弃 / Yangqi).

Why is the Chinese rendering Yangqi (“elevate and discard”) so evocative? Because, as Hegel специально explained in the Science of Logic, the everyday German word aufheben simultaneously carries three seemingly opposite yet indispensable meanings:

  1. To abolish / cancel (Qi / 弃): To break open the calcified shell of the old form and terminate the outdated structure that no longer fits the new environment;
  2. To preserve / retain (Liu / 留): To keep intact the rational kernel and validated capabilities painstakingly developed during the previous stage;
  3. To elevate / transcend (Yang / 扬): To integrate those preserved core assets into a higher-level structural architecture, turning them into organic components of a more capable new system.

To make “Abolish + Preserve + Elevate” immediately concrete, let us trace a classic evolutionary arc in software engineering and systems architecture:

  • Stage 1 (Affirmation: The Monolithic Startup Phase): A three-person startup team, racing to validate product-market fit as fast as possible, writes all its code inside a single codebase (Monolithic Architecture). Its strengths are rapid initial development and effortless deployment.
  • Stage 2 (First Negation: Crisis Erupts and the Monolith Is Broken): As user volume grows to ten million and the engineering team scales to 200 developers, having 200 people edit the same monolithic codebase turns into daily chaos: engineers constantly overwrite one another’s code, and tweaking a minor button requires taking the entire site down for two hours. The CTO initiates the first negation: “Abolish the monolith and migrate to a Microservices Architecture!”
  • When Done as a “Foolish Negation (Bulldoze and Rewrite from Scratch)”: Throwing all 500,000 lines of legacy code—which encode three years of hard-won edge-case business rules—into the trash can and telling 200 engineers to rewrite everything from a blank slate. The result is almost always a disaster: the shiny new system misses dozens of subtle edge-case billing rules, corrupts financial ledgers on launch day, and bleeds the company dry.
  • When Done as Genuine Sublation (Aufheben / Negation of the Negation):
    • Abolish (Qi — Discard the Old Shell): Decisively terminate the outdated deployment form where every module is tightly coupled inside a single process;
    • Preserve (Liu — Retain Core Assets): Carefully extract and preserve the battle-tested business logic, fraud-detection algorithms, and database assets validated over three years of live transactions;
    • Elevate (Yang — Upgrade to a Higher Architecture): Encapsulate those preserved core assets behind clean, standardized APIs as independent service units. By Stage 3, looking at day-to-day developer agility, each small squad can once again ship code independently and rapidly every day, as if they had returned to the nimble three-person startup days of Stage 1—except that the underlying foundation is now a distributed architecture capable of handling hundreds of millions of concurrent requests.

This is what Lenin and Engels meant by describing development not as a flat circle that closes back on itself, but as a “spiral ascent” that seems to revisit earlier stages while standing one full story higher on every turn.

10.2 Xunzi’s “Transforming Inborn Nature Through Conscious Activity” (Hua Xing Qi Wei) and Engels: The Way You Negate the Old Determines Whether the New Can Grow

In Chapter 9 we introduced Engels’s grain of barley; when discussing the “negation of the negation” in Anti-Dühring, Engels formulated an operational maxim of immense practical value:

“I must not only negate, but also sublate the negation again. I must therefore so construct the first negation that the second remains or becomes possible.” (Anti-Dühring, Part I, Chapter XIII)

Underline that second sentence: “I must therefore so construct the first negation that the second remains or becomes possible.”

What does this mean in practice? It means that when you decide to overturn an old habit, reform a legacy department, or move on from a past chapter of life (executing the first negation), the specific manner in which you execute that negation directly determines whether a new crop can grow afterward—or whether you leave behind a scorched salt flat where nothing can ever sprout.

In pre-Qin China, when exploring how human beings can transform their own natural flaws, Xunzi offered an Eastern exemplar of sublation: “Transforming inborn nature and generating conscious activity”（化性起伪） in the Xing’e (“Human Nature Is Wayward”) and Lilun (“Discourse on Ritual”) chapters of the Xunzi.

Many people, hearing that Xunzi argued “human nature is wayward (e),” assume Xunzi advocated eradicating natural human desires (love of profit, sensory pleasure, and ease) like an enemy to be wiped out. Let us read what Xunzi actually wrote in the Xing’e and Lilun chapters:

“Human nature is wayward; its goodness is the result of conscious constructive activity (wei).”（人之性恶，其善者伪也。） (Note: In classical Chinese, 伪 [wei] means conscious human artifice, cultivation, and institutional design, not “fakery.”) “Inborn nature (xing) is the original, unadorned raw material; conscious activity (wei) is the flourishing of pattern and order. Without inborn nature, conscious activity has nothing upon which to work; without conscious activity, inborn nature cannot beautify itself. Only when inborn nature and conscious activity unite are the renown of the sage and the work of unifying the world accomplished.”（性者，本始材朴也；伪者，文理隆盛也。无性则伪之无所加，无伪则性不能自美。性伪合，然后成圣人之名，一天下之功于是就也。）

Read that closing insight carefully: “Without inborn nature, conscious activity has nothing upon which to work; without conscious activity, inborn nature cannot beautify itself. Only when inborn nature and conscious activity unite…”（无性则伪之无所加，无伪则性不能自美。性伪合……）

Xunzi could not have stated it more clearly: innate human desires and instincts (xing) are like uncarved timber and raw jade (benshi caipu); acquired ritual institutions and self-cultivation (wei) are the artisan’s carving and polishing (wenli longsheng). Without the raw material of innate desires and instincts (wu xing), acquired institutions and cultivation have nothing to attach themselves to (wei zhi wu suo jia); yet unless raw desires are channeled and structured (wu wei), raw instinct will never spontaneously turn itself into harmonious order (xing bu neng zi mei).

Place Xunzi’s “Hua Xing Qi Wei” and Engels’s “manner of negation” side by side in our everyday habit change and organizational reform, and the gulf between “destructive negation” and “transformative negation (sublation)” becomes crystal clear:

  • Personal Habit Modification (Breaking the Habit of Late-Night Short-Video Doomscrolling):
    • Destructive Negation (“Without inborn nature, cultivation has nothing to work on”): Ignoring the genuine physiological and psychological need of your brain after ten hours of intense cognitive labor—when your prefrontal cortex is depleted and craves low-effort relaxation and a sense of autonomy (xing)—and swearing a blood oath: “Starting tonight, I will delete all entertainment apps and force myself to memorize dense technical vocabulary from 9:00 to 11:00 p.m.!” Such brute-force negation against human nature rarely survives three days before triggering a violent retaliatory binge until 3:00 a.m.
    • Sublation via Hua Xing Qi Wei: First acknowledge and preserve the legitimate underlying need—“after 9:00 p.m., my fatigued brain genuinely needs low-energy relaxation and dopamine replenishment” (Preserve); abolish the specific toxic vehicle—“lying in bed under the covers scrolling an algorithmic short-video feed whose blue light and variable rewards wreck melatonin and sleep architecture” (Abolish); and elevate by channeling that need into a healthy carrier—“taking a warm shower at 9:30 p.m., setting a 30-minute sleep timer, closing my eyes in the dark, and listening to an engaging history podcast or narrative audiobook” (Elevate—satisfying the brain’s craving for effortless pleasure while letting the eyes and nervous system glide naturally into sleep).

Why did Xunzi emphasize “transforming nature through conscious structure” (化性起伪) rather than brute-force willpower? MIT neuroscientist Ann Graybiel’s research on the basal ganglia shows that habits are encoded as three-part neural chunks (Cue →\rightarrow Routine →\rightarrow Reward); trying to erase an old habit through sheer willpower while leaving the cue and craving intact almost always rebounds once prefrontal energy dips. Combined with Chris Argyris’s Double-Loop Learning (1977), lasting change requires keeping the real cue and underlying need while engineering a concrete new routine and environmental friction design.

10.3 Adorno’s Warning: When Should We Refuse a Forced “Synthesis”?

Having celebrated the power of sublation (Aufheben) and spiral upgrading, in keeping with the dialectical discipline of this handbook, we must now fit a heavy brake pad onto this chariot.

That brake pad comes from a central figure of the twentieth-century Frankfurt School: Theodor W. Adorno.

After witnessing the human catastrophe of two World Wars and the Auschwitz extermination camp, Adorno published his magnum opus Negative Dialectics (Negative Dialektik) in 1966. In it, Adorno leveled a searing moral and epistemological indictment against Hegelian “grand-finale positive synthesis.”

Adorno pointed out that orthodox dialectical systems harbor a dangerous impulse: an eagerness tostuff every suffering, every rupture, and every crushed individual sacrifice into a grand “spiral ascent” script, proclaiming at the end: “Look! Although we passed through the agony of negation in the middle, ultimately everything was reconciled and turned into a higher good in the synthesis!”

Adorno asked with fierce indignation: when an innocent human being is crushed by a flawed institution, or when an avoidable catastrophe occurs, can writing a post-hoc summary report proclaiming that “we withstood the test and achieved a higher level of institutional perfection” ever “sublate” away the agony of the dead?

Adorno’s warning bell serves as two sobering guardrails for our everyday thinking:

  1. Refuse to “Erase Present Real Pain with a Future Happy Ending”: In any organizational reform, post-mortem review, or personal growth narrative, never demand that every disaster be forcibly “synthesized” into a feel-good philosophical elevation. Some blunders are pure stupidity; some losses are irrecoverable wounds. Daring to let a negation remain a negation—looking squarely at the scar and saying honestly, “I made a terrible mistake here, and the harm it caused cannot be beautified”—is ten thousand times more honest than fabricating a “spiral ascent where everything happened to make me stronger.”
  2. The Legitimacy Test for Any “New Synthesis”: How do we judge whether a proclaimed “upgraded synthesis (Stage 3)” is genuine sublation or what Adorno condemned as extorted reconciliation? By a single test: look at the sharpest pain point exposed during Stages 1 and 2 (especially the pain point of the vulnerable party or frontline executor)—was it genuinely resolved structurally in the Stage 3 design, or was it merely silenced in the summary report? If the pain point was simply gagged, that is coercion, not sublation.

Why must every organizational sublation (Aufhebung) guard against turning into top-down tyranny? In Negative Dialectics (1966), Theodor W. Adorno warned against the violence of identity thinking—treating abstract KPIs, averages, or grand strategic blueprints as more real than living individuals (Non-Identity), and amputating any concrete outlier that doesn’t fit the template like the mythical Procrustean Bed. Genuine sublation upgrades the system’s architecture while preserving protected channels for non-standard edge cases and minority feedback.

Common Pitfall: Turning “Negation of the Negation” into a Pendulum That Walks in Circles

In corporate reorganizations and personal planning, the most common counterfeit of the “negation of the negation” is Pendulum Churn:

In Year 1, the company centralizes authority at headquarters (Affirmation); finding frontline response too slow, in Year 2 it decentralizes all authority to regional branches (First Negation); seeing regional branches overspend and lose control, in Year 3 a newly hired VP announces a “Next-Generation Synergistic Centralization Model” (which sounds like a Negation of the Negation, until you open the policy manual and discover it is identical to Year 1’s centralization down to the exact same approval forms, merely reskinned in a new software UI).

How can you tell whether your Stage 3 is a “spiral that climbed to the second floor” or a “flat circle drawn in the mud”? Run a simple “Added Structural Architecture Test”: lay your Stage 3 proposal side by side against your Stage 1 original setup. If Stage 3 neither incorporates the boundary lessons discovered during Stage 2’s failures nor introduces any new technological instrumentation or tiered decision rules, then you are simply flipping a pancake back and forth between Stage 1 and Stage 2.

Thinking Exercises

Case Analysis: Facing the Death of Film, Why Did Kodak Die from “Self-Negation” While Fujifilm Thrived as a Tech Giant?

The Dilemma: In 2000, both Kodak and Fujifilm derived over 60% of their profits from silver-halide photographic film. Within ten years, smartphones and digital sensors wiped out 90% of global film demand. Kodak—which actually invented the first digital camera in 1975—filed for bankruptcy in 2012, whereas Fujifilm grew its market value past $30 billion. Why did two giants on the same cliff meet opposite fates?

  • Reference Analytical Approach:
    1. Kodak’s Error: Clinging to the Shell Until Abstract Negation Hit (Ch. 10.1): Kodak defined its identity around the physical product shell (“selling chemical film rolls”). Fearing that digital cameras would cannibalize its 70%-margin film cash cow, Kodak suppressed its own digital inventions until the market executed a brutal wipeout.
    2. Fujifilm’s Dialectical Sublation (Aufhebung: Abolish, Preserve, Elevate): CEO Shigetaka Komori executed a textbook three-step sublation:
      • Abolish: Ruthlessly shut down 70% of redundant film production lines, accepting that the consumer film era was over.
      • Preserve: Audited the core capabilities accumulated over 70 years of making film—nanoscale antioxidant chemistry, collagen protein control (since photographic emulsion is 50% collagen), and precision optical coatings.
      • Elevate: Re-deployed those exact preserved capabilities into higher-order industries—using film collagen and antioxidant nanotechnology to build the Astalift cosmetics and pharmaceutical division, and using precision optical-film coatings to capture 80% of the global LCD polarizer protective-film market.
