Part VII

★ Chapter 20 Public Discourse: Staying Clear-Headed in an Age of Polarization

If families and workplaces still carry tangible material ties that pull people back toward reason, the moment we unlock our smartphones and step into the public square of social media feeds, short-video comment sections, and group chats, we enter a jungle engineered for maximum Polarization.

Here, any multidimensional public event is flattened within three hours into two warring binary camps: Camp A brands anyone in Camp B morally bankrupt, while Camp B accuses anyone in Camp A of being a paid shill. If anyone dares stand between them and say, “Hold on—this issue involves two distinct structural layers and boundary conditions,” both camps immediately turn their fire on the speaker, jeering them as a “fence-sitter,” “enlightened centrist,” or “apologist.”

Worse still, even complete grammatical sentences are vanishing from online arguments, replaced by a barrage of blood-pressure-spiking single-character combat slang—known across the Chinese internet as the “Ten-Character Online Combat Mantra”: 典 (Diǎn), 孝 (Xiào), 急 (Jí), 蚌 (Bèng), 乐 (Lè), 赢 (Yíng), 润 (Rùn), 麻 (Má), 寄 (Jì), 摆 (Bǎi) (and their Western internet cousins: “Cope,” “Seethe,” “Shill,” “Bootlicker,” “NPC,” “Lulz,” “Touch grass,” “Doomer”).

As the final chapter of our practical applications, we confront a survival question every modern citizen faces daily: in a digital ecosystem where recommendation algorithms and emotional contagion relentlessly herd human beings into binary diodes, how can we avoid being swallowed by the noise—neither becoming an angry megaphone for tribal rage, nor retreating into numb, sneering cynicism?

20.1 How Recommendation Algorithms Eradicate “Calibration (Shizhong)” and “Concrete Context”

Before blaming internet users for “having low literacy or failing to understand dialectics,” let us examine the digital ecosystem through a dialectical systems lens: the extreme polarization of contemporary online discourse is not happening because human brains suddenly mutated; it is happening because the underlying technical architecture of information distribution (engagement-maximizing recommendation algorithms and the attention economy) systematically punishes dialectical nuance and richly rewards one-sided extremism.

In Chapter 7 we studied Timely Equilibrium (Shizhong—calibration and concrete context), and in Chapter 4 we studied Xunzi’s “Laying Out All Things Side by Side (Jian Chen Wan Wu)”. Yet healthy dialectical thinking has three inherent physical traits: it requires paragraph length to state boundary conditions, it requires the reader to recruit slow deliberation (System 2) to weigh competing factors, and its conclusions are measured rather than delivering an instant dopamine hit of righteous outrage.

What, by contrast, is the objective function coded into modern short-video and social-media recommendation algorithms? Completion rate, dwell time, and immediate engagement velocity (likes, quote-retweets, and furious comment wars).

Trained on billions of user interactions, the algorithm quickly discovers an iron law of human neurobiology:

  • A rigorous 3,000-word analysis explaining that “Policy X yields net benefits under boundary condition A, but requires compensatory subsidies under boundary condition B” gets a 4% completion rate and a quiet comment section;
  • A 15-second clip set to pounding suspense music, headlined “SHOCKING! Group X Is Destroying Everything!” with all prior context edited out to leave only a 3-second flash of confrontation, triggers an adrenaline spike across a million viewers in three seconds and ignites 50,000 furious comments.

Consequently, twenty-four hours a day, the algorithmic filter performs two structural operations on human reality:

  1. Context Stripping: Taking a concrete event that occurred at a specific time, in a specific place, under a complex chain of prior causes (Chapter 7’s Time and Position), chopping off its head and tail, draining out all conditional context, and turning it into an abstract moral totem designed to trigger tribal reflexes;
  2. Echo-Chamber Welding: The moment you linger for five extra seconds on a one-sided take, the algorithm feeds you fifty consecutive posts with the exact same slant—coding Xunzi’s “blinded by a single corner and darkened to the overarching pattern” directly into your feed until you are viscerally convinced that “every sane person on earth agrees with me, and anyone who disagrees is either evil or brain-dead.”

Understanding how this algorithmic centrifuge works gives you your first layer of digital immunity: whenever a 15-second clip or viral screenshot on your phone makes your blood boil and makes your thumb itch to share or firing off an angry reply, freeze your thumb in midair for three seconds and ask yourself one question: “What happened in the five minutes BEFORE this clip started and the five minutes AFTER it was cut?”

Why do online discussions reliably degenerate into warring camps? Legal scholar Cass R. Sunstein proved the Law of Group Polarization (Going to Extremes, 2009): when like-minded people deliberate inside an algorithmically filtered echo chamber, they do not converge toward the center—instead, informational cascades and reputational one-upmanship push the group toward a far more extreme position than its members held initially, while collapsing what psychologist Philip Tetlock calls Integrative Complexity (the ability to perceive multiple dimensions and conditional trade-offs) down to primitive binary tribalism.

★ 20.2 How to Stand Firm on a “Structural Perspective” in a Polarized Debate—Without Falling into Muddy Compromise

Confronted with a ferocious black-and-white shouting match online, where should a dialectical thinker stand?

Many people’s first instinct is: “Both camps are too extreme, so I’ll stand dead center at the 50% mark and say both sides have a point and both sides should be nicer.”—Never do that! As we proved in Section 14.1, handing out fifty lashes to each side without investigating causal structure or power asymmetry is Confucius’s xiangyuan (muddy compromise), and deserves every bit of the ridicule it gets as “spineless fence-sitting.”

Where a genuine dialectician stands in public debate is not at the flat arithmetic midpoint between Left and Right on the ground, but one dimension higher—on the Structural Perspective.

To make the difference between “muddy compromise” and a “structural perspective” unmistakable at a glance, let us compare four distinct modes of public commentary on the exact same viral news event:

[Event Background]: To crack down on traffic violations, a municipal government abruptly announces a strict campaign imposing heavy fines and a mandatory three-day account suspension on food-delivery riders caught speeding or riding the wrong way. On Day 1 of the crackdown, during a torrential rainstorm, several riders terrified of late-delivery penalties get into heated roadside altercations with traffic police, and clips of the confrontations go viral nationwide.

Within an hour, public commentary splits into four distinct modes:

  1. Mode 1: Binary Tribal Combatants (Single-Sided Blindness)
    • Extreme Pole A: “Delivery riders run red lights and ride on sidewalks all day long like road menaces! Fine them into oblivion and ban their accounts permanently!”
    • Extreme Pole B: “Hardworking gig workers risk their lives in pouring rain to earn pennies per order, and heartless traffic cops are bullying the poor!”
    • Diagnosis: Each camp grabs one real fragment of reality (pedestrian safety vs. gig-worker livelihood), demonizes the other human group on the street, and locks into an endless moral shouting match.
  2. Mode 2: Xiangyuan Muddy Compromiser (Section 14.1 Pseudo-Dialectic)
    • Script: “Everyone has it tough—riders work hard to make a living, and traffic police work hard to keep roads safe. Let’s all take a step back: riders should ride more slowly, and pedestrians and police should be more forgiving. Let’s fill the world with love!”
    • Diagnosis: Sounds warm and benevolent, yet is 100% useless—because the underlying physical mechanism forcing riders to speed in the rain hasn’t changed by a millimeter, and tomorrow’s rainstorm will produce the exact same collisions.
  3. Mode 3: Bureaucratic Boilerplate Reciter (Section 15.2 Pseudo-Dialectic)
    • Script: “Overall urban traffic governance remains stable. We must dialectically handle the relationship between road safety order and gig-economy employment—both defending the red line of traffic safety and safeguarding the legitimate rights of flexible workers, compacting primary responsibility across all parties.”
    • Diagnosis: Hollow ceremonial prose that leaves the unresourced “both… and…” double bind sitting right on the wet asphalt.
  4. Mode 4: Genuine Dialectical “Structural Analyst” (The Approach of This Handbook)
    • Analytical Trajectory:
      1. Look Past Surface Combatants to Map the Full Causal System (Chapter 11): The two parties colliding physically at the intersection are “riders” and “traffic police / pedestrians”—yet hiding invisibly behind the smartphone screen sits the player holding overwhelming governing dominance (the Principal Aspect of the Contradiction, Chapter 12): the delivery platform’s dispatch algorithm and penalty structure.
      2. Expose Unilateral Cost-Shifting Under Power Asymmetry (Section 14.5): The platform algorithm compresses promised delivery times to the physical limit where a rider cannot complete the route on time without speeding or cutting corners—allowing the platform to pocket 100% of the commercial premium of “30-minute ultra-fast delivery,” while externalizing 100% of the physical double bind (“obey traffic laws and get docked half a day’s pay for lateness, OR speed and risk police fines and crashes”) onto riders and pedestrians.
      3. Export Structural Mechanism Fixes (Chapters 6 & 7): Fining only individual riders treats the symptom while sparing the cause. A genuine structural solution brings the invisible Principal Aspect back into the legal accountability loop: municipal regulators audit the platform’s dispatch algorithm directly via API—whenever an order’s assigned delivery window is shorter than the physical travel time calculated at legal e-bike speed (15 km/h15\text{ km/h}) plus average red-light waits, the platform company is fined automatically for algorithmic inducement of traffic violations; and rainstorm/snow warnings automatically trigger a mandatory 15-minute weather extension switch (Jing and Quan).

Compare Mode 2 (Muddy Compromise) against Mode 4 (Structural Analysis): Mode 2 stands between two exhausted boxers in the ring pleading with them to smile at each other; Mode 4 looks up and asks: “Who built this ring and wrote the payout rules that force these two people to trade blows in the rain?” That is the real power of dialectical thinking in the public square.

How do we escape self-interested bias when evaluating a heated public controversy? Political philosopher John Rawls proposed the Veil of Ignorance in A Theory of Justice (1971): before judging whether a rule is fair, imagine you do not yet know which role you will be dealt once the veil lifts—whether you will be the platform algorithm engineer, the exhausted delivery rider, the late customer, or the small restaurant owner. Pairing Rawls’s role-blindness test with a structural audit of who captures the upside vs. who bears the tail risk instantly lifts your analysis above tribal mudslinging.

★ 20.3 Character-by-Character Dissection and Countermeasures for the Ten Online Combat Slang Terms (“典、孝、急、蚌、乐、赢、润、麻、寄、摆”)

In today’s online comment sections and group chats, even when you write out a rigorous, well-sourced structural analysis like Mode 4 above, you will frequently run into a baffling reply: the commenter doesn’t address a single number in your post or point out a single flaw in your causal chain; instead, they toss a single Chinese character into the replies—“典” (Diǎn), “急” (Jí), “孝” (Xiào), “乐” (Lè), or “赢” (Yíng) (the exact functional equivalents of English internet replies like “NPC script,” “Cope and seethe,” “Corporate bootlicker,” “Lulz,” or “Tired of winning”).

What exactly are these ten ubiquitous single-character tokens—collectively dubbed the “Ten-Character Online Combat Mantra” (典、孝、急、蚌、乐、赢、润、麻、寄、摆)? Why are they so contagious that within a few years they have reduced countless public forums into single-syllable mud-slinging pits?

Before dissecting all ten characters one by one, let us examine the evolutionary game theory and pragmatic essence of this vocabulary through our dialectical lens:

In Chapter 15 we saw that Bureaucratic Dialectics evolved inside corporate and administrative hierarchies as a defensive linguistic shield—using wordy, hedged clauses (“generally controllable, both… and…”) to evaporate personal accountability and block falsification. Online combat slang evolved in the exact opposite habitat—anonymous, high-hostility, low-trust, hyper-fragmented comment feeds—as ultra-low-cost Anti-Dialogue Weaponry.

Look at the game-theoretic return-on-investment (ROI) inside an anonymous online argument:

  • If you play by the rules of rational discourse—spending 30 minutes checking primary sources, compiling statistics, and writing a 500-word structured argument (high cognitive investment)—a troll can spend 1 second replying with a single mocking character, derailing the thread at near-zero cost while your 500 words of concrete propositions expose multiple surface areas for nitpicking.
  • Conversely, if a commenter abandons reasoning altogether and fires off a single character devoid of subject, predicate, or factual assertion (such as “典” or “急”), their typing cost approaches zero (1 second), and because a single interjection contains zero falsifiable empirical propositions, you have no logical handle to refute! Better still, that single character executes an instant pragmatic hijack—shifting the spotlight from “Is your argument supported by evidence?” to “Look at this clown getting emotional / reciting a script!”, handing the drive-by commenter an instant hit of cheap superiority as they stroll away.

Thus, Bureaucratic Boilerplate and Online Single-Character Slang are two faces of the exact same anti-rational coin: bureaucratic prose uses verbose hedging to make accountability evaporate, while combat slang uses one-syllable labels to induce brain-death in dialogue.

Understanding why these tokens evolved does not mean surrendering to them. On the contrary, once we strip open the pragmatic sleight of hand, psychological defense mechanism, and logical fallacy inside each of the ten characters, we can spot them instantly, disarm them with a single surgical sentence, and immunize our own minds against linguistic decay.

Below, we group the ten characters into six functional categories and conduct a full forensic dissection, live scenario reconstruction, and counter-script design for each:

Category I. “典” (Diǎn — “So Classic” / “Classic NPC Script”): Abusing Pattern Recognition to Bypass Substantive Refutation

  • Pragmatic Trap & Fallacy: Short for “classic textbook specimen.” The user commits Dismissal by Categorization (Bulverism)—conflating “how often an argument has been stated before (Frequency)” with “whether its evidence and logic are actually true (Truth Value).” Basic truths (like gravity, supply and demand, or crash-test physics) recur frequently because they describe objective constraints; slapping a “典” sticker on a detailed post lets a commenter pretend they have “seen through the script” without refuting a single fact.
  • Dialectical Counter-Script: Never reply “You’re the classic one!” Calmly ask them to name the error: “Could you specify which exact template my data copied from, and point out which of the three factual premises above is wrong? If you can’t point to a single factual error, dropping a single syllable ‘典’ to dodge checking the numbers might be the most overused template online.”

Category II. “急” (Jí — “You’re Triggered / Salty / Mad”): Swapping Emotional Attribution for Factual Truth

  • Pragmatic Trap & Fallacy: Whenever you write a detailed, earnest reply, the troll drops “He’s triggered (急了)”—executing a covert Topic Shift + Kafka Trap: moving the spotlight from “Is Proposition P true?” to “Is Speaker A emotionally agitated?” If you stay silent, they claim victory; if you reply to explain you aren’t mad, they crow “Look, he wrote another paragraph—definitely triggered!”
  • Dialectical Counter-Script: Never defend your emotional state; grant the label with light irony and pin the spotlight back onto the unanswered fact: “Sure, let’s say I’m furious and typing with my blood pressure at 180—now that we’ve settled my mood, can you answer why the sample size in Table 2 of your claim was missing 40% of the data?”

Category III. “孝” (Xiào — “Filial Bootlicker / Shill”) and “润” (Rùn — “Love It or Leave It / Just Run”): Motive Assassination and Revoking Standing to Speak

  • Pragmatic Trap & Fallacy: If you defend any concrete merit of an organization, product, or policy, trolls yell “孝” (“Paid shill / corporate bootlicker”); if you criticize any concrete defect, trolls yell “润” (“Why don’t you emigrate / quit then?”). Both commit Ad Hominem Motive Assassination and a False Binary Loyalty Test—assuming a person must either worship an entity 100% blindly or hate it 100% and leave, outlawing the civic middle ground of “bounded, fact-based evaluation.”
  • Dialectical Counter-Script: Use Unbundled Accounting to break the binary: “Recognizing that Product X’s battery thermal management passes safety standards while criticizing its buggy infotainment UI is basic engineering hygiene—not being a ‘shill’ or a ‘hater.’ Can we evaluate the thermal test data on its own merits?”

Category IV. “乐” (Lè — “Pure Lulz / Spectator Cynicism”) and “蚌” (Bèng — “Can’t Hold Back My Laugh”): Dissolving Serious Reasoning with Nihilistic Sneers

  • Pragmatic Trap & Fallacy: Derived from “laughing at the spectacle” and “holding back a laugh until my face splits.” By adopting the posture of an detached, ironic spectator who treats earnest problem-solving as a clown show (Cynical Meta-Detachment), the commenter shields their own ego from ever taking a falsifiable stand—since someone who only laughs on the sidelines can never be proven wrong.
  • Dialectical Counter-Script: Recognize that performative cynicism is aimed at an audience, not truth. Either ignore it completely, or leave a calm structural note for silent readers: “Laughing on the sidelines is easy because spectators don’t pay the repair bill; for those actually solving the bottleneck, here are the three actionable parameters.”

Category V. “赢” (Yíng — “Winning So Hard I’m Numb / Tired of Winning”): Using Totalizing Irony to Erase Calibrated Local Progress

  • Pragmatic Trap & Fallacy: Originally a healthy satire of triumphalist propaganda that boasts of victory on every front; when overused as a reflexive knee-jerk sneer (“Winning again / Win-numb!”), it mutates into Reverse Totalizing Nihilism—treating any verified local improvement or technical breakthrough as fake propaganda unless 100% of unrelated societal problems are solved simultaneously.
  • Dialectical Counter-Script: Apply Chapter 7’s Dimensional Partitioning: “Mocking fake omni-directional bragging is fair, and at the same time, verifying that solar module conversion efficiency rose by 2.4% in Lab Test B does not claim that unrelated housing or employment pressures have vanished. Let’s keep separate ledgers separate.”

Category VI. “麻 / 寄 / 摆” (Má / Jì / Bǎi — “Numb / GG Doomed / Let It Rot”): Defensive Forfeiture and Exiting the Table

  • Pragmatic Trap & Fallacy: Whereas the first seven terms attack opponents, 麻 (Numb), 寄 (GG Doomed), and 摆 (Let it rot) are white flags of Learned Helplessness and Self-Handicapping (Berglas & Jones, 1978). By extrapolating a local setback into “100% irreversible cosmic doom,” the speaker protects their ego (“I didn’t fail because I lacked skill—I failed because the whole game is rigged and I wasn’t even trying!”).
  • Dialectical Counter-Script: Toward anonymous strangers spamming “Doomed (寄了)” online, recognition is the finish line—do not argue back. Toward teammates or your own inner voice, unbundle the uncontrollable macro cycle from the controllable micro margin (Ch. 12): “Macro growth slowing from 25% to 5% means tailwinds cooled, not that 100% of players drop to zero. Let’s list the two micro-variables we still control 100% before Friday.”

The Meta-Strategy for Online Slang: Pull the Exchange from the “Signal Layer” Back to the “Argument Layer” Once—and If It Won’t Budge, Walk Away

Stepping back to survey all ten characters above, you will see that despite their different costumes, structurally they all perform the exact same cognitive operation: dragging human communication down from the Argument Layer (where we examine empirical facts, causal mechanisms, probability weights, and boundary conditions) into the Status & Identity Signal Layer (where participants hurl tribal status labels: NPC, triggered, shill, clown).

When confronted with this downward drag, a clear-headed dialectical thinker follows three iron rules:

  1. Never Fight Slang with Slang: If someone replies to you with “急” (Triggered) and you fire back “典” (Classic NPC) or “孝” (Shill), the two of you have merged into the exact same mud pit at IC=1\text{IC} = 1.
  2. Extend Exactly ONE “Pull Back to the Argument Layer” Test: Using the counter-scripts above, calmly, politely, and without profanity ask once for the specific factual error or logical break. Remember: when you post that calm counter-question in a public thread, your primary audience is rarely the troll who typed the single character—it is the 95% of silent, reasonable readers watching the thread who are hungry for a clear cognitive anchor.
  3. Ruthlessly Filter Your Interlocutors (Save Your Sharpest Steel for Worthy Minds): Chapter 4’s Socratic dialogue and Steelmanning require real cognitive energy. Only if the other person responds to your first prompt by dropping the slang and putting concrete reasons or data on the table have they earned a second reply from you; if their second reply is another single-character sneer or personal insult, close the tab immediately—protecting your finite attention for people and problems that matter is the highest respect you can pay your own life.

Common Pitfall: Don’t Mistake Harmless Self-Deprecating Humor Among Friends for Malicious Combat Slang

When applying Section 20.3, remember Chapter 7’s calibration (Shizhong).

When a close friend vents in a private chat after a grueling week of overtime, “I’m totally numb / doomed this week (麻了/寄了),” or when an author finishes writing a thorough 500-word evidence-backed analysis and caps it off with a light self-deprecating joke—that is normal emotional decompression and human humor.

There is only one criterion separating harmless humor from toxic anti-dialogue slang: is the word a pinch of seasoning alongside a substantive argument, or is it a gag used to replace argument and stuff the other person’s mouth shut?

Thinking Exercises

Case Analysis: Detoxifying and Reconstructing a Viral Public Controversy

The Dilemma: A viral video shows an exhausted food-delivery rider arguing with a university campus security guard who barred his electric scooter at the gate, causing the rider to hurl the lunchbox onto the ground and burst into tears. Within two hours, the comment section splits into two vicious mobs: Camp A doxxes the security guard as a “power-tripping bully trampling the working class,” while Camp B attacks the rider as “unprofessional, violent, and playing the victim”—followed by trolls spamming “典” (Classic script), “急了” (Triggered), and “麻了” (Numb/Doomed).

  • Reference Analytical Approach:
    1. Filter Out Slang Trolling & Refuse the Binary Hero/Villain Frame (Ch. 20.1 & 20.3): Both the rider (earning 5 RMB per order against a strict 35-minute algorithm countdown) and the security guard (earning 4,200 RMB/month under a campus rule that fines him 200 RMB if a scooter enters after a recent pedestrian collision) are low-power frontline executors trapped at the bottom of two incompatible rule systems.
    2. Apply Rawls’s Veil of Ignorance & Structural Tension Audit (Ch. 20.2):
      • Why did two frontline workers collide at the gate? Because the delivery platform’s routing algorithm calculated delivery time straight to the dorm building door as if the campus gate barrier did not exist, while the university logistics office banned scooters at the perimeter without installing a takeout relay locker at the gate.
      • Structural Solution: Instead of firing the guard or penalizing the rider, install insulated smart takeaway lockers at the campus gate and update the platform’s GPS endpoint so riders mark orders delivered the moment food enters the gate locker—eliminating the artificial collision between campus pedestrian safety and rider delivery timers.
