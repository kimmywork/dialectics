Part II

★ Chapter 4 Socrates and Xunzi's Jiebi (Dispelling Obsessions): Breaking the Grip of One-Sided Bias

While Chapter 3’s reductio ad absurdum helps us catch logical flaws inside a single chain of reasoning, in real life we face an even more pervasive cognitive hurdle: Every single sentence the other person says is a 100% verified local fact, and their internal logic is completely consistent—yet they are staring exclusively at one leg of the elephant and building an entire master plan around it.

In the West, Socrates spent his life questioning fellow Athenians in the marketplace to show how partial and unexamined our proudest convictions really are; in late Warring States China, Xunzi wrote the first systematic treatise in human intellectual history diagnosing the pathology of one-sided cognitive bias: Chapter 21 of the Xunzi, “Dispelling Obsessions” (Jiebi 解蔽).

★ 4.1 Socratic Questioning: We Think We Understand Until We Are Asked Three Questions in a Row

In Plato’s early dialogues (such as the Laches, Euthyphro, and Meno), Socrates demonstrates a conversational method that left Athenian notables both fascinated and exasperated—known to posterity as the Socratic Elenchus (cross-examination / refutation).

Let us watch the classic scene in Plato’s Laches: Socrates asks the famous Athenian general Laches a seemingly elementary question—“What is courage?”

Having fought battles for half his life, General Laches considers the question child’s play and answers off the cuff:

  • First Definition (190e): “That is not hard to state: if a man is willing to stand his ground in the ranks, face the enemy, and not flee, you may be sure he is courageous.”
  • Socrates’ First Probe (Introducing Counterexamples to Expose an Overly Narrow Definition, Laches 191a–c): “Well spoken, Laches. Yet I hear that Scythian cavalrymen fight by wheeling and retreating on horseback rather than standing still; and at the Battle of Plataea, the Spartans themselves, facing the Persian wicker-shield wall, deliberately fell back until the Persian ranks broke formation in pursuit, then turned around like cavalry to rout them and win the war. Were those warriors who fought while retreating cowards?”
  • Laches pauses, admitting he mistook “one specific tactical posture of heavy hoplite infantry” for “courage itself,” and offers a broader second definition.
  • Second Definition (192b): “Then courage is a certain endurance (steadfastness) of the soul across all things.”
  • Socrates’ Second Probe (Introducing a Value Premise to Narrow an Overly Broad Definition, 192c–d): “Laches, courage is surely among the noblest things. Yet endurance accompanied by folly (such as stubbornly charging into a pointless trap) is harmful and ignoble, so it cannot be courage. Therefore, only wise endurance (endurance accompanied by knowledge and prudence) can count as courage, right?” Laches agrees.
  • Third Probe (Uncovering the Deep Paradox / Aporia, 192e–193e): Socrates immediately follows “wise endurance” one layer deeper:
    1. “Suppose a patient with severe pneumonia begs for water and food, and his physician—knowing through medical expertise that giving food or water will kill him—‘wisely and steadfastly’ refuses the patient’s pleas. Would anyone call the physician’s ‘wise endurance’ in treating the illness courage?” Laches shakes his head: “No, nobody calls that courage.”
    2. “Now consider war or diving into a deep well: Soldier A possesses expert military calculation (or expert diving skill) and knows the odds are overwhelmingly in his favor, so he endures and dives in; Soldier B lacks that expertise, knows his skill is inferior and the danger far greater, yet grits his teeth and dives in anyway. Tell me honestly, Laches: to ordinary intuition, which of the two men seems more courageous?”
    • Laches blurts out in surprise: “I have to admit, the less-skilled man who braves the greater unknown risk looks more courageous than the confident expert!”
    • Socrates spreads his hands: “Look where we have arrived: a moment ago we agreed that ‘courage must be endurance guided by wisdom and knowledge,’ yet when we test it against concrete cases, we deduce that ‘the less knowledge one has and the rasher the risk one takes, the braver one appears’—which means foolish endurance turns out to be courage! Our own premises have collided and shattered each other!”

Within three rounds, a seasoned general who has commanded armies for decades realizes to his astonishment that the word “courage” he uses every day is a fuzzy muddle in his own head.

Notice the exact operation Socrates performs here: He does not hand Laches a pre-packaged textbook definition; instead, by introducing real counterexamples from varied contexts, he peels back the narrow, rigid local experience (“standing in a hoplite phalanx = courage”) that Laches had mistaken for the whole truth.

That is the second deconstructive scalpel of dialectical thinking: whenever we feel 100% certain about a concept (what counts as “success,” a “good employee,” or “putting the user first”), proactively crash three extreme edge-case scenarios against it to see whether we have mistaken a single local habit for universal truth.

★ 4.2 Xunzi’s Jiebi (Dispelling Obsessions): “The Common Flaw of Humanity Is Being Blinded by One Corner and Darkened to the Great Pattern”

If Socrates dissolved one-sided stubbornness through one-on-one dialogue, the late Warring States philosopher Xunzi (c. 313–238 BCE) wrote a panoramic pathological diagnosis of why human minds fall into one-sidedness in Chapter 21 of the Xunzi, “Dispelling Obsessions” (Jiebi 解蔽).

The chapter opens with sixteen characters that cut straight to the bone of human cognition:

“The common flaw of all people is that they become obsessed (blinded) by one corner of a matter, and so remain in the dark about its great overarching pattern.”
（凡人之患，蔽于一曲，而暗于大理。）

What does “blinded by one corner (bi yu yi qu 蔽于一曲) and darkened to the great pattern (an yu da li 暗于大理)” mean? Yi qu is a single local angle, a partial slice of reality; da li is the overarching systemic structure and full causal network. Xunzi is pointing out that humanity’s greatest cognitive malady is rarely total ignorance; rather, it is letting the one local corner we happen to see block our vision of the whole system.

Why do even brilliant minds fall into “one-corner blindness”? Xunzi immediately lists a comprehensive etiology of cognitive bias:

“Hence the sources of obsession (blindness): desire blinds, aversion blinds; the beginning blinds, the end blinds; the distant blinds, the near at hand blinds; breadth blinds, shallowness blinds; antiquity blinds, the present blinds. Whenever myriad things differ, none fails to act as a blindfold to the others—this is the common malady of the mind’s art.”
（故为蔽：欲为蔽，恶为蔽，始为蔽，终为蔽，远为蔽，近为蔽，博为蔽，浅为蔽，古为蔽，今为蔽。凡万物异则莫不相为蔽，此心术之公患也。）

Translated into modern cognitive psychology and behavioral economics, Xunzi cataloged humanity’s core cognitive biases twenty-three hundred years ago:

  • Desire blinds, aversion blinds (yu wei bi, wu wei bi): Seeing only the upsides of what we crave and only the flaws of whom we dislike (modern psychology’s motivated reasoning and halo/horn effects);
  • The beginning blinds, the end blinds (shi wei bi, zhong wei bi): Getting locked into first impressions, or judging an entire process solely by its final outcome (primacy effect, anchoring, and outcome bias);
  • The distant blinds, the near at hand blinds (yuan wei bi, jin wei bi): Seeing only immediate short-term gains while ignoring long-term hazards, or spinning ten-year utopian blueprints while ignoring tomorrow’s cash-flow survival (hyperbolic discounting and myopia vs. abstraction bias);
  • Antiquity blinds, the present blinds (gu wei bi, jin wei bi): Either worshiping legacy traditions as sacred (“the good old days”) or chasing the latest trend while dismissing all hard-won historical experience.

Better still, Xunzi did not stop at abstract psychology; he named names across the Hundred Schools of Thought, delivering the earliest “Diagnostic Audit of One-Sided Blindness Among Elite Thinkers” in intellectual history:

“Mozi was blinded by utility (yong) and did not know cultural refinement (wen); Songzi was blinded by desire (yu) and did not know actual attainment (de); Shen Dao was blinded by law (fa) and did not know worthy talent (xian); Shen Buhai was blinded by positional power (shi) and did not know discerning wisdom (zhi); Hui Shi was blinded by words (ci) and did not know substantive reality (shi); Zhuangzi was blinded by Nature (tian) and did not know human agency (ren).”
（墨子蔽于用而不知文，宋子蔽于欲而不知得，慎子蔽于法而不知贤，申子蔽于势而不知知，惠子蔽于辞而不知实，庄子蔽于天而不知人。）

Savor the depth of Xunzi’s diagnosis:

  • Mozi saw commoners starving and freezing, so he championed “practical utility and frugality (yong).” Was he wrong? Not at all! Yet when he elevated “bare utility” to the sole absolute standard and demanded the abolition of all music, art, and ritual refinement (bu zhi wen), society became barren and austere;
  • Shen Dao saw the instability of personal rule, so he championed “impartial legal codes (fa).” Was he wrong? Not at all! Yet when he believed dead statutes could govern a state automatically without cultivating capable, ethical administrators (bu zhi xian), the law became a rigid corpse;
  • Hui Shi dissected the logic of words and concepts (ci) so obsessively that he lost touch with empirical realities (bu zhi shi); Zhuangzi revered spontaneous alignment with Nature (tian) so deeply that he neglected human institutional effort to transform the world (bu zhi ren).

Xunzi then delivers his famous summation:

“Therefore, if one speaks of the Way solely from utility, it is reduced entirely to profit-seeking; if one speaks of the Way solely from desire, it is reduced entirely to gratification (qie 嗛); if one speaks of the Way solely from law, it is reduced entirely to mechanical technique; if one speaks of the Way solely from positional power, it is reduced entirely to expediency; if one speaks of the Way solely from words, it is reduced entirely to disputation; if one speaks of the Way solely from Nature, it is reduced entirely to passive compliance. These several approaches are each one corner of the Way (dao zhi yi yu). As for the Way itself, its substance is constant yet it exhausts all changes; a single corner is insufficient to lift the whole. People of partial knowledge observe one corner of the Way and fail to recognize it as just a corner; thus they take it to be sufficient and dress it up, throwing themselves into confusion within and misleading others without, blinding subordinates from above and blinding superiors from below—this is the disaster of cognitive obstruction (bise zhi huo).”
（故由用谓之道，尽利矣；由欲谓之道，尽嗛矣；由法谓之道，尽数矣；由势谓之道，尽便矣；由辞谓之道，尽论矣；由天谓之道，尽因矣。此数具者，皆道之一隅也。夫道者，体常而尽变，一隅不足以举之。曲知之人，观于道之一隅，而未之能识也，故以为足而饰之，内以自乱，外以惑人，上以蔽下，下以蔽上，此蔽塞之祸也。）

(Note: Here “speaking of the Way from utility” corresponds to Mozi reducing the Way to utilitarian calculation; “speaking of the Way from desire” corresponds to Song Jian reducing the Way to appeasing psychological desires; the remaining four clauses correspond to Shen Dao’s law, Shen Buhai’s power, Hui Shi’s words, and Zhuangzi’s Nature.)

This passage uncovers the truth behind the vast majority of high-level arguments in human affairs: When smart people in boardrooms, academia, or public policy argue until they are blue in the face, rarely is anyone making things up out of thin air; each person is holding a genuine shard of reality (“one corner of the Way”). The tragedy is that each person mistakes the shard in their own hand for the entire mirror (“taking it to be sufficient and dressing it up”), and uses it to attack everyone holding the other shards.

How, then, do we cure this “one-corner blindness”? Xunzi prescribes two remedies: “Emptiness, Unity, and Stillness” (xu yi er jing 虚壹而静) and “Laying out the myriad things side by side and suspending the balance-beam in the center” (jian chen wan wu er zhong xuan heng yan 兼陈万物而中县衡焉)—do not let preconceptions clog your mind (xu), lay all competing perspectives and opposing evidence out on the table at once (jian chen wan wu), and weigh their relative proportions across the whole system.

★ 4.3 Confucius’s “Harmony Without Sameness”: Why Dissenting Opinions Are a Cognitive Necessity

Since every one of us—constrained by our professional training, departmental incentives, and past experience—is inevitably “blinded by one corner” (the CFO naturally blinded by cost risk, the VP of Sales by revenue expansion, the CTO by architectural elegance), how can we possibly see the blind spot on the back of our own head when making personal or team decisions?

There is only one answer: You must borrow the eyes of the person standing in the opposite position to serve as your rearview mirror.

That is the deep cognitive value of Confucius’s maxim in Analects 13.23 (Zilu): “The exemplary person seeks harmony (he) and not sameness (tong); the petty person seeks sameness and not harmony.” （君子和而不同，小人同而不和。） Many people treat “harmony without sameness” as a polite social platitude about being nice to others. From the standpoint of dialectical thinking, however, dissent (“not sameness”) is not annoying static that we grudgingly tolerate; it is the life-saving oxygen that keeps us from suffocating inside our own blind spots.

In Western philosophical and debate training, there is a core discipline that is the exact opposite of the Straw Man fallacy, known as Steelmanning:

  • Strawmanning: Caricaturing and weakening your opponent’s argument into its dumbest possible form so you can knock it down easily and feel smart;
  • Steelmanning: Proactively supplying the missing premises and strongest empirical evidence for your opponent’s position—reinforcing their argument into an armor-plated “steel man”—and only then asking: Faced with this strongest possible version of the opposing view, where does my original plan need patching?

As Karl Popper emphasized, human knowledge never grows by piling up a thousand confirming examples that flatter our existing beliefs; it grows by surviving the severest possible attempted refutations and revising our errors in the process. The next time you hear an objection that makes you bristle, before asking whether the other person has it in for you, look through Xunzi’s lens and ask yourself: “Are they standing in the exact corner of the room that my own eyes cannot see?”

Why does an echo chamber of individual stars lose to a cognitively diverse team? Complex-systems scientist Scott E. Page proved the Diversity Prediction Theorem (The Difference, 2007): Collective Crowd Error = Average Individual Error −- Prediction Diversity. In a yes-man team where everyone shares the same mental model, prediction diversity drops to zero and the group inherits 100% of the leader’s blind spots; bringing in dissenting perspectives makes positive and negative biases cancel out, driving collective error far below any solo genius’s error.

Common Pitfalls: Dispelling Everyone Else’s Obsessions While Never Examining Your Own

After reading Xunzi’s Jiebi and Socrates’ elenchus, the easiest trap is to use them as a diagnostic label gun aimed exclusively at other people—pointing at the sales lead in a meeting and saying “You are blinded by short-term revenue,” pointing at legal and saying “You are blinded by rigid clauses,” while quietly assuming that you alone sit on Mount Olympus beholding the “Great Pattern.”

Xunzi warned explicitly that people who think they have mastered the whole and go around lecturing others on their partiality are often trapped in the deepest blindness of all. Remember: No human being stands naturally at the center of the “Great Pattern” by default. Genuine unblinding (jiebi) always begins by placing your own professional filter and self-interest on the table first—admitting up front, “Because I trained as an engineer, I naturally overvalue architectural elegance and undervalue distribution channels.” Only then does your field of vision truly open up.

Case Analysis: The Technical Founder’s “Superior Product Blind Spot”

The Dilemma: Founder Old Zhou, a veteran acoustics engineer, spent two years building a lossless smart speaker with studio-grade harmonic fidelity (THD<0.02%THD < 0.02\%). Yet six months after launch, a rival lifestyle brand with mediocre audio specs outsold him 8:18:1. Old Zhou fumed at his product team: “Consumers today are completely brainwashed by flashy marketing—they can’t even tell real engineering quality from garbage!”

  • Reference Analytical Approach:
    1. Uncover the Hidden Reference Frame (Ch. 5.1): “Good product” is not an isolated physical constant—it exists only in relation to a specific user scenario. Old Zhou defined “good” inside an acoustic lab frame (sitting motionless in a soundproof room), whereas 90% of buyers evaluated speakers inside a living-room social frame (voice wakeup reliability from 5 meters away, instant multi-room pairing, and warm industrial design).
    2. Inject Heterogeneous Perspectives via Page’s Theorem (Ch. 5.2): Because Old Zhou’s 12-person product board consisted entirely of male audio engineers, their prediction diversity was zero. Bringing in industrial designers and non-audiophile household users revealed that his app required a painful 7-step Wi-Fi setup that drove a 38% return rate.
    3. Qi–Zheng Conversion (Ch. 5.3): Keep studio-grade acoustic fidelity as his diferenciated edge (Qi), while immediately fixing the baseline user-experience table stakes—1-click Bluetooth/Wi-Fi onboarding and tactile physical controls (Zheng).
